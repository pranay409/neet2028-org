Cell Division for NEET: Mitosis vs Meiosis — Everything That Comes in the Exam

By Sneha Iyer · 📅 October 27, 2026 · ⏱ 11 min read · 🎯 NEET Biology

Cell division is not just a chapter—it's the foundation of your entire Biology score. If you ask any NEET topper, mitosis and meiosis will appear in at least 2–3 questions every year, sometimes disguised as diagram-based problems or sneaky MCQs about chromosome behavior. NCERT Chapter 10 (Cell Cycle and Cell Division) typically carries 4–5 marks directly, but understanding this chapter unlocks answers in genetics, reproduction, and even human physiology questions. Yet most students rush through it, memorizing phase names without understanding why cells divide the way they do. In the next 11 minutes, we're going to change that.

Why Cell Division Matters for NEET: The Real Exam Pattern

Before diving into mitosis vs meiosis, understand where these questions actually appear on your answer sheet. In the last 5 NEET cycles, cell division questions have been distributed like this:

  • Direct 2-marker questions: "Name the phase where sister chromatids separate" or "Draw and label a metaphase plate in meiosis II."
  • 5-marker diagram questions: "Describe the events during prophase I of meiosis. Why is it longer than prophase of mitosis?"
  • Crossover questions: Genetics problems that assume you know recombination happens in prophase I—if you don't, the entire genetics calculation falls apart.
  • Case-based questions: "A cancer cell divides uncontrollably. Explain how checkpoint failure during mitosis could cause this."

This means your strategy isn't just to memorize phases—it's to deeply understand the biological purpose of each division type. Mitosis creates identical cells for growth and repair. Meiosis creates genetic variation for reproduction. That's the real test NEET asks.

Mitosis: The Quick Breakdown for Growth and Repair

Mitosis is straightforward once you lock in the core idea: one cell becomes two identical cells, both with the same chromosome number as the parent. Here's what you absolutely must know for the exam:

The Four Phases of Mitosis (In Context)

Prophase: Chromosomes condense (you see them under a microscope now), the nuclear envelope breaks down, and the centrioles move to opposite poles forming the spindle fibers. This is the longest phase of mitosis—about 50% of mitotic time. Students often miss that the spindle fibers form outside the nucleus as the nuclear envelope dissolves.

Metaphase: Chromosomes line up at the metaphase plate (the cell's equator) attached to spindle fibers. This is the shortest phase. Why does NEET care? Because this is when you can count chromosomes most clearly. A 2-marker question might ask: "In a cell with 2n=4, how many chromosomes are visible in metaphase?" Answer: 4 (not 8—each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids, but it's counted as one unit at this stage).

Anaphase: Sister chromatids separate. Spindle fibers shorten, pulling identical chromatids to opposite poles. The key phrase: "V-shaped" or "J-shaped" chromosomes appear as chromatids are dragged. This visual detail shows up in diagram questions.

Telophase: Nuclear envelopes reform around the separated chromatids (now called chromosomes). Spindle fibers disappear. Cytokinesis (cell division) usually begins here.

⚠️ Common NEET Mistake:

Students confuse "chromosome" and "chromatid" across phases. Remember: Before anaphase, a chromosome = 1 + 1 sister chromatids (2 units, 1 chromosome). After anaphase, each chromatid is now a separate chromosome. If a cell has 2n=6, metaphase shows 6 chromosomes but 12 chromatids. Anaphase ends with 12 chromosomes (6 moving each way). NEET loves testing this distinction.

Meiosis: The Complex Division That Creates Genetic Variation

Meiosis is where students typically lose points because it involves two divisions and requires understanding why reduction matters. Here's the framework NCERT uses (and NEET tests):

Meiosis I: The Reduction Division (Reductional Division)

This is the critical division. A cell with 2n chromosomes divides into two cells with n chromosomes. The reason? Gametes (sex cells) must have half the chromosome number so that when they fuse during fertilization, the chromosome count is restored.

Prophase I: This is the longest and most complex phase of any cell division. Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) and crossing over (recombination) occurs. This is where genetic variation originates. Students must know: crossing over happens between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. If a 5-marker question asks "Why is prophase I longer than prophase of mitosis?", your answer is: "Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis), and crossing over occurs, exchanging genetic material. This requires the cell to spend extra time organizing and recombining DNA." Textbook answer that scores full marks.

Metaphase I: Bivalents (paired homologous chromosomes) align at the cell's equator, not individual chromosomes. Count carefully: if 2n=4, you see 2 bivalents in metaphase I, not 4 individual chromosomes.

Anaphase I: Homologous chromosomes separate (not sister chromatids). Each chromosome still consists of two sister chromatids. This is a key difference from anaphase of mitosis. One chromosome from each pair moves to each pole.

Telophase I & Cytokinesis I: Two daughter cells form, each with n chromosomes (but each chromosome still has two sister chromatids). The cell is now haploid but still duplicated.

Meiosis II: The Equatorial Division (Just Like Mitosis)

Meiosis II resembles mitosis but works on haploid cells. Sister chromatids separate, producing four haploid gametes total from the original diploid cell.

  • Prophase II: Chromosomes condense again in the two haploid cells.
  • Metaphase II: Individual chromosomes (now n) align at the metaphase plate.
  • Anaphase II: Sister chromatids separate—just like mitotic anaphase.
  • Telophase II & Cytokinesis II: Four haploid daughter cells result.

Head-to-Head: Mitosis vs Meiosis (The Comparison NEET Loves)

Feature Mitosis Meiosis
Number of Divisions 1 2
Daughter Cells Produced 2 4
Chromosome Number 2n → 2n (diploid) 2n → n (haploid)
Homologous Pairing No Yes (Prophase I)
Crossing Over No Yes (Prophase I)
Genetic Variation Identical daughter cells Four unique gametes
