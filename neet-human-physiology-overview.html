Human Physiology for NEET: The 8 Systems You Must Master

By Arjun Mehta · 📅 November 05, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Biology

Human physiology is worth approximately 60–70 marks in NEET, spread across three major topics: digestion, respiration, circulation, and excretion. This makes it the single largest scoring chapter in the entire NEET Biology syllabus. Yet most students treat it as a memorization exercise, missing the exam patterns that repeat year after year. If you get physiology wrong, a 600+ score becomes nearly impossible. The good news: physiology has clear structure, predictable question types, and logic that connects every system. Master these 8 systems with the right strategy, and you'll unlock 50+ marks that most students leave on the table.

1. The Digestive System: Enzyme Pathways & Absorption (15–18 marks)

NCERT Chapter 16 is the most frequently tested physiology chapter. NEET consistently asks 3–4 questions from digestion, with at least one numerical on enzyme activity or starch/protein breakdown. The exam doesn't care if you can recite the mouth-to-anus journey verbatim—it tests whether you understand substrate-enzyme specificity, optimal pH ranges, and nutrient absorption sites.

What Actually Gets Tested

  • Enzyme specificity: Questions like "Which enzyme breaks down sucrose?" or "At what pH does pepsin work optimally?" appear in nearly every exam session. Know the substrate, enzyme name, and pH for: salivary amylase (starch, pH 6.5–7), pepsin (protein, pH 1.5–2), trypsin (protein, pH 8–8.5), and lipase (fat, pH 7–8).
  • Absorption sites: The small intestine, particularly the jejunum and ileum, is where 90% of nutrient absorption occurs. NEET asks about microvilli, villi, and brush borders as adaptations for absorption. Memorize which nutrient absorbs where: glucose and amino acids in duodenum/jejunum (active transport), fats in lacteals (lymphatic), and water/salts throughout the small intestine.
  • Gastric vs. intestinal phase: At least one question per year compares HCl secretion, gastrin release, or chyme movement between stomach and small intestine phases. The stomach holds food for 3–4 hours; the small intestine for 2–3 hours.
  • Common mistake: Students confuse bile (emulsifies fats, no enzymes) with pancreatic lipase (actually breaks down fats). Bile is not an enzyme; it's a physical emulsifier. This distinction appears in nearly every alternate-year NEET session as a statement-based question.
⚡ Critical Gap Most Students Miss

NEET tests the chemical composition of digestive juices, not just their location. Practice questions that ask: "Which of these is NOT in pancreatic juice?" (common wrong answer: HCl, which is gastric juice only). The NCERT table on page 286–287 of Chapter 16 is gold. Most toppers memorize this table; most average students skip it.

2. Respiratory System: Gas Exchange & Hemoglobin Binding (12–14 marks)

NCERT Chapter 17 covers respiration, and NEET asks 2–3 questions here, almost always focused on oxygen-hemoglobin binding, partial pressures, and respiratory mechanics. Anatomical questions (larynx position, bronchiole count) rarely appear; functional questions dominate.

Oxygen Dissociation Curve & Boyle's Law

The oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve is tested in at least 1 in 3 NEET papers. Questions ask: "At what pO₂ is hemoglobin 50% saturated?" (Answer: ~26 mmHg, called P50). You need to know how CO₂, H⁺, and temperature shift the curve to the right (promoting oxygen release in tissues). This is called the Bohr effect, named after Christian Bohr. In tissues with high metabolic rate (muscles, brain), blood is warmer and more acidic—so hemoglobin releases oxygen more readily. This connects physiology to real biology: your working muscles get oxygen when they need it most.

Respiratory Volumes & Capacities

NEET asks 1–2 numerical questions on tidal volume, vital capacity, or FEV₁/FVC ratio. Standard values to memorize: tidal volume = 500 mL, vital capacity = 3500–4500 mL, total lung capacity = 6000 mL. If a question states "vital capacity is reduced to 60% of normal," calculate it as 60% × 4000 = 2400 mL. These aren't random numbers—they're clinically meaningful. A vital capacity below 80% suggests respiratory disease, which NEET uses for case-based questions.

3. Circulatory System: Blood Flow & Pressure Gradients (16–20 marks)

Chapter 18 is the heaviest physiology chapter by mark weight. NEET asks 4–5 questions here, with special focus on cardiac cycle, blood pressure regulation, and vessel structure-function relationships.

The Cardiac Cycle & Pressure Changes

Most students memorize "atrial systole, ventricular systole, diastole" without understanding the pressure sequence that drives blood flow. In reality, blood flows from high pressure to low pressure. During ventricular systole, left ventricular pressure rises to ~120 mmHg, forcing the aortic valve open. During diastole, aortic pressure stays ~80 mmHg while ventricular pressure drops below 80 mmHg—so the aortic valve snaps shut. This prevents backflow. NEET asks: "When do the aortic and pulmonary valves open?" (Answer: Early ventricular systole, when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic/pulmonary pressure). Understanding pressure gradients transforms this from pure memorization into logical deduction.

Heart Sounds & Murmurs

The first heart sound (S1, "lub") is caused by atrioventricular valve closure (mitral and tricuspid). The second heart sound (S2, "dub") is caused by semilunar valve closure (aortic and pulmonary). A heart murmur is an abnormal sound caused by turbulent blood flow—either a valve is leaking (regurgitation) or stenotic (narrowed). NEET asks: "A diastolic murmur suggests which valve is defective?" (Answer: Aortic or pulmonary, because diastole is when semilunar valves should close). This logic appears in 1–2 NEET papers yearly.
