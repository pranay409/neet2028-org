Plant Physiology for NEET: Photosynthesis and Respiration Deep Dive

By Divya Reddy · 📅 November 08, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Biology

Plant physiology—specifically photosynthesis and respiration—consistently accounts for 8-12 marks in NEET, spread across both MCQs and case-based questions. Yet most students treat these chapters as abstract theory, memorizing equations without understanding the mechanism. This is exactly why they freeze when a question asks about the light-dependent reactions in chloroplasts or the role of ATP in cellular respiration. Here's the truth: once you understand these processes as energy management systems, the chapter becomes predictable, and your score becomes bulletproof.

1. Photosynthesis: The Energy Architecture You Must Master

NCERT Chapter 13 (Photosynthesis in Higher Plants) is deceptively detailed. NEET examiners love testing three specific areas: the light-dependent reactions in the thylakoid, the light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle) in the stroma, and the factors affecting photosynthetic rate. The mistake 80% of students make is memorizing the Z-scheme or the Calvin cycle diagram without asking why each step exists.

Light-Dependent Reactions: The Power Plant

Think of Photosystem II (PS II) and Photosystem I (PS I) as two power stations. Water enters PS II and gets split by the oxygen-evolving complex—this is where the 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ reaction occurs. NEET almost always asks: where exactly does photolysis happen? Answer: in the lumen of the thylakoid, specifically at the manganese cluster in PS II. The electrons knocked free from water flow through the electron transport chain, pumping protons across the thylakoid membrane. This creates a proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis. Then PS I re-energizes those electrons to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH. The entire cycle is orchestrated to produce one outcome: ATP and NADPH (the energy carriers) and O₂ (the waste). Understanding this as an energy extraction system makes it unforgettable.

Recent NEET trends show more questions on the location of photosystems (PS II in the appressed regions of stacked thylakoids, PS I in unstacked regions) and the role of plastoquinone and cytochrome b6f complex. These aren't random details—they're testable facts that appear in case-based questions.

The Calvin Cycle: Carbon Fixation Engine

NEET students often mix up the Calvin cycle with other metabolic pathways. Let's anchor it: RuBP (5-carbon) + CO₂ → 3-PG (3-carbon) is catalyzed by RuBisCO, the most abundant protein on Earth. This is the carbon fixation step. Then 3-PG is reduced to G3P using the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. For every 6 G3P molecules produced, 5 are recycled to regenerate RuBP, and 1 exits the cycle to form glucose. This 3:5:1 ratio is NEET gold. Questions often ask: what happens if light reactions stop? Answer: ATP and NADPH dry up, 3-PG accumulates, and RuBP decreases because it can't be regenerated. This isn't just theory—it's a logical prediction that NEET tests repeatedly.

Key Mistake Students Make:

Assuming the Calvin cycle requires light. It doesn't. It requires ATP and NADPH, which are produced by the light reactions. If you place a plant in darkness, the light reactions stop immediately, but the Calvin cycle stutters to a halt within seconds due to lack of ATP and NADPH. NEET loves this distinction. Remember: Calvin cycle is light-independent, not light-proof.

2. Factors Affecting Photosynthesis: The Practical Testing Ground

This section consistently appears in NEET as multi-step questions. The three factors are light intensity, CO₂ concentration, and temperature. Each has a distinct relationship with the photosynthetic rate, and NEET examiners test whether you can interpret graphs or predict outcomes under different conditions.

Light Intensity and the Compensation Point

The light compensation point is the light intensity where the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration—meaning net gas exchange is zero. NEET questions ask: at what light intensity does a plant stop showing negative gas exchange? Or: a shade plant has a lower light compensation point than a sun plant—why? Understanding this concept requires knowing that shade plants are efficient at low light (they have more chlorophyll and thylakoid stacking) but saturate quickly, while sun plants need higher light intensities to reach maximum photosynthetic rate. Recent questions have asked students to interpret photosynthesis rate vs. light intensity curves and identify the light saturation point.

CO₂ Concentration and RuBisCO Kinetics

As CO₂ increases, the photosynthetic rate increases until it plateaus (around 0.05% CO₂ in normal conditions). The limit shifts based on temperature and light availability. NEET has asked: if temperature increases but light remains constant, why does the photosynthetic rate initially increase then decrease? The answer involves enzyme kinetics—RuBisCO works faster at higher temperatures initially, but photorespiration (the oxygenase activity of RuBisCO) also increases, creating a net loss. This connects to C3 vs. C4 vs. CAM pathways, which are barely covered in NCERT but occasionally appear in NEET questions.

3. Respiration: The Mirror Image, But Crucial Differences

NCERT Chapter 14 covers Respiration in Plants, and this is where many students stumble because they try to memorize glycolysis, Krebs cycle, and electron transport chain as isolated pathways. Instead, view respiration as the reverse of photosynthesis—a controlled breakdown of glucose to extract energy (ATP) and reducing power (NADH, FADH₂). The key word is controlled: if this were combustion, all the energy would release as heat. Because it's enzymatic, the energy is captured in chemical bonds.

Aerobic Respiration: The Complete System

Glycolysis (in cytoplasm): 1 glucose → 2 pyruvate, net 2 ATP, 2 NADH. Krebs cycle (in mitochondrial matrix): 1 acetyl-CoA → 2 CO₂, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP. Electron transport chain (in inner mitochondrial membrane): NADH and FADH₂ are oxidized, proton gradient drives ATP synthase, producing ~30-32 ATP per glucose. The theoretical yield is ~38 ATP, but real plants achieve 30-32 ATP due to membrane leakage and transport costs.
