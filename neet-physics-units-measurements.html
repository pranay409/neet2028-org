Units and Measurements for NEET: The Chapter Everyone Underestimates

By Karan Malhotra · 📅 October 12, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Units and Measurements is the first chapter of NCERT Physics Part 1, and most students make the same critical mistake: they skip it. The logic seems sound—it's "just definitions and conversions, nothing like circular motion or thermodynamics." This careless assumption costs students 2–3 guaranteed marks every year, and far worse, it builds a weak foundation for dimensional analysis problems that appear across mechanics, waves, and electricity. If you can't confidently derive units or check the dimensional consistency of an equation, you'll stumble on numerical problems that top scorers finish in 90 seconds.

Why This Chapter Matters More Than You Think

NEET exams consistently test units and measurements through three distinct question patterns: direct unit conversions (1 question), dimensional analysis (1–2 questions), and measurement precision/errors (1 question). That's 3–4 marks of relatively straightforward points—if you've studied correctly. But most students haven't. They memorise SI units without understanding why they matter, skip dimensional analysis practice, and then panic when faced with a question like "The dimensions of electrical resistance are [ML²T⁻³A⁻²]. Express this in terms of power and current."

NCERT Chapter 2 (Units and Measurements) in Physics Part 1 covers exactly this ground. The chapter introduces the SI system, the concept of fundamental and derived units, dimensional analysis, and measurement errors—each a distinct testing territory. Knowing these topics saves you from both careless mistakes and conceptual gaps that resurface later when you study electromagnetic induction or fluid mechanics.

Mastering SI Units and the Metric System

The SI (International System of Units) is the language NEET uses. You must know seven fundamental quantities: length (metre), mass (kilogram), time (second), electric current (ampere), temperature (kelvin), luminous intensity (candela), and amount of substance (mole). Every other unit is derived from these seven.

Here's what separates confident students from confused ones: they don't just memorise the units—they understand the relationships. For example, force is derived as kg·m·s⁻² because F = ma and you multiply the units of mass (kg) by acceleration (m·s⁻²). Energy is then kg·m²·s⁻² because work is force times distance. Once you see these chains, unit conversion becomes automatic rather than a recipe-following exercise.

Common Conversions NEET Tests Repeatedly

  • Energy conversions: 1 calorie = 4.18 joules, 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules. These appear in thermodynamics and modern physics questions.
  • Pressure conversions: 1 atm = 101325 Pa. Essential for fluid mechanics and kinetic theory.
  • Astronomical units: 1 parsec, 1 AU (astronomical unit). Used rarely but students panic when they see unfamiliar units.
  • Velocity of light and Planck's constant: c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s, h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. Appear in optics and atomic physics.

The strategy here is simple: don't memorise random conversions. Instead, solve 20–30 numerical problems that require unit conversions. You'll naturally internalise the ones that matter. NCERT provides these problems at the end of Chapter 2—solve every single one and create a personal reference sheet.

Dimensional Analysis: The Hidden Superpower

Dimensional analysis is where the real intelligence of this chapter lives. It's not just about checking if an equation is correct (though that's useful). It's about deriving formulas you've never seen before, verifying complex expressions, and catching your own mistakes in numerical calculations.

The fundamental principle: physical quantities can only be added or compared if they have the same dimensions. You can't add 5 metres to 5 seconds—they're dimensionally incompatible. This principle cascades into powerful applications:

Deriving Unknown Formulas Using Dimensional Analysis

Suppose you know that the period T of a simple pendulum depends on its length L, the mass m of the bob, and gravitational acceleration g. Using dimensional analysis, you can derive T = 2π√(L/g) without needing to remember the formula.

Set up the equation: T = k · L^a · m^b · g^c (where k is a dimensionless constant). Write the dimensions: [T] = [L]^a · [M]^b · [LT⁻²]^c. Expand: T¹M⁰L⁰ = L^a · M^b · L^c · T^(-2c). Matching exponents for each dimension gives you a = 1/2, b = 0, c = –1/2. Therefore, T ∝ √(L/g). This technique works for dozens of NEET problems and saves enormous amounts of guesswork.

⚠️ Mistake Students Make:

Many students treat dimensional analysis as a "checking tool" only—verifying if an answer is dimensionally correct. This is backward. Use it as a problem-solving weapon. When you see a formula you don't remember, derive it using dimensions. When a numerical answer seems wrong, check if the dimensions work. This habit catches conceptual errors before they cost you marks on exam day.

Measurement Errors: The Overlooked Points

The final section of NCERT Chapter 2 covers absolute errors, relative errors, and percentage errors. This seems trivial until NEET asks: "Two resistances R₁ = (100 ± 2) Ω and R₂ = (50 ± 1) Ω are connected in series. What is the total resistance and its error?" Students freeze because they've never practiced error propagation.

Here's the framework that works: when you add or subtract quantities, absolute errors add. When you multiply or divide, relative (or percentage) errors add. For a series circuit: R_total = R₁ + R₂ = 150 Ω, and ΔR_total = ΔR₁ + ΔR₂ = 2 + 1 = 3 Ω. So the answer is (150 ± 3) Ω.

These questions appear 1–2 times per NEET paper and usually in combination with other chapters (circuits, waves, mechanics). Master them now, and you'll solve multi-part problems with confidence later.
