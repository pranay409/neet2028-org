Chemical Bonding for NEET: VSEPR Theory and Hybridization Explained

By Priya Nair · 📅 October 23, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

Chemical bonding and molecular structure form the backbone of NEET chemistry—and they're worth 6-8 marks on the actual exam. Most students memorize hybridization types without understanding the "why," then panic when they see an unfamiliar molecule. VSEPR theory and hybridization concepts appear not just as direct questions, but woven into organic chemistry, inorganic reactions, and even biochemistry problems. If you're scoring below 80% on bonding questions, the gap isn't memory—it's conceptual clarity. This guide will show you how toppers approach these topics, what NCERT actually expects, and the three patterns that repeat in every NEET paper.

Understanding VSEPR Theory: The Foundation

VSEPR stands for Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory, covered in NCERT Chemistry Class 11, Chapter 4. The core principle is deceptively simple: electron pairs (both bonding and lone pairs) repel each other, and molecular geometry arranges itself to minimize this repulsion. But here's what separates NEET toppers from average scorers: understanding that the theory predicts electron geometry first, then molecular geometry—and the exam tests both.

For a NEET question asking "What is the shape of SO₂?", the electron geometry is tetrahedral (4 electron pairs around sulfur). But the molecular geometry is bent (because one pair is lone), which affects polarity and reactivity. This distinction appears in 3-4 mark questions almost every year.

The Steric Number Formula

Steric number = Number of bonded atoms + Number of lone pairs. This single calculation unlocks 80% of bonding geometry questions:

  • Steric number 2: Linear (e.g., CO₂, BeCl₂)
  • Steric number 3: Trigonal planar (e.g., BF₃, NO₃⁻) or bent if 1 lone pair (e.g., SO₂)
  • Steric number 4: Tetrahedral (e.g., CH₄, CCl₄) or trigonal pyramidal if 1 lone pair (e.g., NH₃) or bent if 2 lone pairs (e.g., H₂O)
  • Steric number 5: Trigonal bipyramidal (e.g., PCl₅) with axial and equatorial positions
  • Steric number 6: Octahedral (e.g., SF₆, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻)

NEET examiners often test whether you know that in trigonal bipyramidal structures, lone pairs occupy equatorial positions first (to minimize 90° repulsions over 120° ones). Missing this costs marks on XeF₂ and ClF₃ problems.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Ignoring Lone Pairs

Students often count only bonded atoms, forgetting that lone pairs take up space in the electron geometry. SO₂ has 2 bonded O atoms and 1 lone pair on S—steric number 3. Its electron geometry is trigonal planar, but molecular geometry is bent. This distinction directly affects whether SO₂ is polar (it is) versus linear molecules like CO₂ (which aren't).

Hybridization: Types and Recognition Patterns

Hybridization appears in NCERT Chapter 4 and ties directly to bonding angles and molecular properties. Rather than memorizing hybridization, NEET toppers work backwards: identify the steric number and electron geometry, then the hybridization follows automatically. This matters because exam questions ask you to predict molecular properties (polarity, bond strength, reactivity) based on hybridization type—not just name the type.

The Hybridization-Steric Number Link

  • sp (steric number 2): Linear, 180° bond angles. Examples: acetylene (HC≡CH), CO₂, BeCl₂
  • sp² (steric number 3): Trigonal planar, 120° angles. Examples: ethene (C=C double bond), NO₃⁻, BF₃
  • sp³ (steric number 4): Tetrahedral, 109.5° angles. Examples: methane (CH₄), ethane (C-C single bond), diamond lattice
  • sp³d (steric number 5): Trigonal bipyramidal, mixed 90° and 120° angles. Example: PCl₅, AsF₅
  • sp³d² (steric number 6): Octahedral, 90° angles. Examples: SF₆, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻, [CoCl₆]³⁻

NEET frequently tests carbon hybridization in organic compounds. When you see a carbon atom with 4 single bonds, it's sp³. A carbon in a C=C double bond is sp². A carbon in C≡C is sp. This becomes crucial in reactions where bond strength or reactivity depends on hybridization—sp-hybridized carbons form shorter, stronger bonds than sp³.

Hybridization and Bond Angles

Bond angles change with hybridization due to electron pair repulsion. An sp³ hybridized carbon has 109.5° angles (tetrahedral), but ammonia (NH₃, sp³ on N but with 1 lone pair) has only 107° because lone pairs repel more strongly. NEET tests this in multi-step problems: "Arrange NH₃, H₂O, and CH₄ by bond angle" requires knowing that CH₄ has 109.5°, NH₃ has 107°, and H₂O has 104.5°. The pattern: more lone pairs compress the angle further.

VSEPR and Hybridization in Exam Patterns

Over the last 5 years, NEET has repeatedly asked three types of bonding questions. Recognizing these patterns lets you allocate study time wisely.

Pattern 1: Predicting Molecular Geometry (2-3 marks)

You're given a molecule like SO₂, XeF₄, or NH₄⁺ and asked to name its shape. Solution: Calculate steric number, identify geometry. Don't memorize—derive. For SO₂: S has 6 valence electrons, forms 2 S=O bonds (uses 4), leaves 2 unpaired → 1 lone pair. Steric number = 2 (bonded) + 1 (lone) = 3 → trigonal planar electron geometry, bent molecular geometry.

Pattern 2: Comparing Polarity or Bond Angles (3-4 marks)

Questions like "Which is more polar: NH₃ or PH₃?" require you to compare both structure and electronegativity. NH₃ (sp³, tetrahedral electron geometry, trigonal pyramidal with 1 lone pair) is polar because the lone pair's electron density isn't cancelled by N-H bond dipoles. PH₃ is also pyramidal but less polar because P is less electronegative than N. Toppers first check geometry, then electronegativity difference.

Pattern 3: Hybridization and Reactivity (4-6 marks)

These appear in organic chemistry units. "Explain why ethyne is more acidic than ethene." Answer: Ethyne has sp-hybridized carbons (s-character ~50%), making the C-H bond more polar and H more acidic. Ethene has sp² carbons (s-character ~33%), so its C-H bond is less polar. This connects bonding theory directly to reactivity—exactly what NCERT emphasizes.

✓ Exam-Winning Strategy: Draw Electron Dot Structures First

Before applying VSEPR or predicting hybridization, always draw the Lewis structure showing all valence electrons. This step catches 90% of mistakes. For PCl₅: P is central, forms 5 P-Cl bonds, has no lone pairs. Steric number = 5, so sp³d hybridization, trigonal bipyramidal geometry. Without the structure, students often miscalculate.

Advanced Applications: Resonance and Formal Charge

NCERT Chapter 4 also covers resonance structures, which affect the actual geometry and bonding character. NO₃⁻ resonates among three structures—each N-O bond is neither single nor double, but approximately 1.33 order. This partial character affects bond length and strength, tested in comparison questions. Calculate formal charges on each atom to confirm the most stable resonance structure, then use that geometry for your answer.

Formal charge = Valence electrons − (bonded electrons/2) − lone pair electrons. In NO₃⁻, nitrogen has 5 valence electrons, forms 3 bonds to oxygens, and has 0 lone pairs →
