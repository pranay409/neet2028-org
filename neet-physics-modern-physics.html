Modern Physics for NEET: Photoelectric Effect to Nuclear Physics

By Arjun Mehta · 📅 November 09, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Modern physics is where NEET separates the 500+ scorers from the rest. This topic spans NCERT Chapters 11, 12, and 13 and typically carries 8–12 marks across 3–4 questions. The challenge isn't the concepts—they're intuitive once you understand the physics. The real problem is that most students memorize formulas without grasping why photoelectrons get ejected, why nuclei emit radiation, or how atoms exist with electrons circling a nucleus. The moment an exam question deviates slightly from your textbook example, you panic. This guide will change that.

Understanding the Photoelectric Effect: The Foundation

The photoelectric effect (NCERT Chapter 11) is where modern physics begins for NEET students. Light hits a metal surface, electrons shoot out. Simple. But here's what separates 3-mark answers from full marks: understanding why this doesn't fit Newtonian mechanics.

Classical physics predicted that dimmer light would take longer to eject electrons (energy accumulates). Reality? Dimmer light never ejects electrons below a threshold frequency, no matter how long you wait. Brighter light (more photons, same frequency) ejects electrons faster. Einstein's quantum explanation: light comes in packets (photons) of energy E = hf. Each photon hits an electron in an all-or-nothing event. This single insight solves every photoelectric question.

The work function (W or Φ) is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron. If a photon's energy (hf) exceeds this, the excess becomes kinetic energy: KE_max = hf - W. Most NEET questions test this equation or ask you to identify what changes kinetic energy (frequency changes it; intensity doesn't). A critical graph question: plot KE_max versus frequency—it's linear with slope h (Planck's constant). This is high-yield for numerical problems.

Common Mistakes in Photoelectric Problems

Students often confuse work function with threshold frequency. Work function is energy (in eV or Joules); threshold frequency is the minimum frequency (in Hz). They're related by W = hf₀, but they're different quantities. NEET loves tripping you here. Also, stopping potential (V_s) is directly related to max kinetic energy by eV_s = KE_max. If you find stopping potential, multiply by charge to get KE.

⚡ High-Yield Insight:

Photoelectric effect always appears as either (1) a direct calculation using hf = W + KE, (2) a graph interpretation question, or (3) a conceptual question about what doesn't change when you adjust light properties. Memorize this: changing intensity changes photocurrent (more photons → more electrons ejected per second), but doesn't change stopping potential (each electron's energy stays the same). This is tested nearly every year.

Bohr's Atomic Model and Energy Levels

NCERT Chapter 12 introduces the Bohr model for hydrogen. After photoelectric effect, students often struggle because Bohr's model seems disconnected—it's actually a natural extension of quantum ideas. Bohr proposed that electrons orbit nuclei only at specific radii where angular momentum is quantized: L = mvr = n(h/2π), where n = 1, 2, 3... (principal quantum number).

Why? Because standing waves. Imagine an electron as a wave circling the nucleus. Only wavelengths that fit perfectly (circumference = n × wavelength) create stable orbits. This connects wave-particle duality from Chapter 11 directly to atomic structure. For NEET, memorize the formula for energy levels in hydrogen: E_n = −13.6 eV / n². Negative energy means the electron is bound (you need positive energy to remove it—that's ionization energy). Transition energy between levels (say, n=2 to n=1) is ΔE = 13.6 × (1/1² − 1/2²) = 10.2 eV. This energy is released as a photon with frequency f = ΔE/h.

NEET commonly asks: "What frequency of photon is emitted when an electron jumps from n=3 to n=1?" Set up the energy difference, divide by h, and calculate. Or: "What wavelength of light would be absorbed to jump from n=1 to n=2?" Same method. These are nearly guaranteed questions, often worth 2–3 marks.

Why Bohr's Model Still Matters

Modern quantum mechanics replaced Bohr with orbitals (fuzzy probability clouds), but NEET sticks with Bohr because it's calculable without advanced math. Bohr correctly predicts hydrogen spectrum and ionization energy. For multi-electron atoms, you use screening/shielding concepts—inner electrons partially shield outer electrons from the nucleus, so effective charge decreases.

Nuclear Physics and Radioactivity: The High-Yield Section

NCERT Chapter 13 covers the nucleus, binding energy, mass defect, and radioactive decay. This chapter accounts for 5–8 marks on NEET and is often tested conceptually and numerically. Start with what a nucleus is: protons (positive charge) and neutrons (neutral) packed incredibly tightly. The strong nuclear force holds them together—it's strong but short-range. This explains why heavy nuclei become unstable (repulsive Coulomb force between protons grows faster than binding energy).

Mass defect (Δm) is the difference between the sum of individual nucleon masses and the actual nucleus mass. This "missing" mass is binding energy (BE = Δm × c² via Einstein's equation). High binding energy per nucleon means stable nucleus. The binding energy curve (nucleons vs. BE per nucleon) shows Fe-56 is most stable—this is why nuclear fusion (combining light nuclei) and fission (splitting heavy nuclei) both release energy.

Radioactivity: unstable nuclei decay to become stable. Three types dominate NEET:

  • Alpha decay: Nucleus emits a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons). Mass number A decreases by 4; atomic number Z by 2. Example: U-238 → Th-234 + He-4.
  • Beta decay: Neutron converts to proton (emitting electron + antineutrino). A stays same; Z increases by 1. Example: C-14 → N-14 + e⁻.
  • Gamma decay: High-energy photon emitted (nucleus de-excites). A and Z unchanged. Often follows alpha or beta.

The decay constant (λ) and half-life (T₁/₂) are linked: T₁/₂ = 0.693/λ. Activity (decay rate) follows N(t) = N₀ e^(−λt). NEET loves half-life problems: "After 3 half-lives, what fraction remains?" Answer: (1/2)³ = 1/8. Or: "How many half-lives for 99.9% decay?" Solve 0.001 = (1/2)^n
