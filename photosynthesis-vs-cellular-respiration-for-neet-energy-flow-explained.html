Photosynthesis vs Cellular Respiration for NEET: Energy Flow Explained

By Rohan Verma · 📅 November 28, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Biology

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are the two pillars of bioenergetics—and they appear in nearly every NEET paper. Between NCERT Class 11 Chapter 13 (Photosynthesis in Higher Plants) and Class 12 Chapter 7 (Respiration in Plants), you'll face 4–6 mark questions that demand crystal-clear understanding of how energy flows through cells. Most students treat these topics separately, but the NEET examiner tests them as inverse processes—and that's where confusion kills marks. This guide will show you exactly how these processes mirror each other, the common traps, and the high-yield comparison frameworks that toppers use.

Understanding the Fundamental Energy Flow in Living Systems

Energy enters the biological world through photosynthesis. Plants trap light energy and convert it into chemical energy stored in glucose. This is an endergonic process—it requires energy input. Without photosynthesis, there would be no usable energy in the biosphere.

Cellular respiration is the reverse: it breaks down glucose and releases that stored chemical energy in the form of ATP, which powers every cellular function. This is an exergonic process—it releases energy. No ATP production means cell death.

The core concept NEET tests: Photosynthesis builds molecules; respiration breaks them down. One stores energy; the other retrieves it. Understanding this inverse relationship is non-negotiable.

From NCERT Class 11, you'll recall that photosynthesis occurs in chloroplasts and has two stages: the light-dependent reactions (in thylakoids) and the light-independent reactions or Calvin cycle (in stroma). Cellular respiration, covered in Class 12, occurs in mitochondria and includes glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and the electron transport chain. Examiners love asking why energy yield differs between anaerobic and aerobic pathways—and that's often where students drop marks.

Photosynthesis Breakdown: What NEET Needs You to Know

Photosynthesis is summarized by the equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. But memorizing this formula without understanding the stages is a recipe for getting only 1–2 mark questions correct.

Light-Dependent Reactions (Thylakoid Membrane)

These reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane of the chloroplast. Here's what happens: chlorophyll absorbs photons and electrons from water molecules become excited. As electrons move through Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI), energy is released to pump protons into the thylakoid lumen, creating a concentration gradient. This gradient drives ATP synthase to produce ATP. Simultaneously, NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH. Both ATP and NADPH are products of the light reactions and are crucial for the next stage.

NEET frequently asks: "Where is NADPH formed?" (Answer: in PSI). "Why is water split?" (Answer: to replenish electrons lost from PSII and release O₂). Missing these details costs marks on 2-mark and diagram-based questions.

Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)

The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma and uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to fix CO₂ into glucose. The cycle has three phases: fixation (CO₂ combines with RuBP via RuBisCO enzyme), reduction (3-PG is reduced to G3P using ATP and NADPH), and regeneration (G3P regenerates RuBP). For every three turns of the cycle, one G3P exits to form glucose.

Examiners test: Why is RuBisCO the most abundant enzyme on Earth? (Because it's needed in massive quantities due to its low catalytic efficiency). What happens to the Calvin cycle when light is suddenly removed? (ATP and NADPH production stops, so 3-PG accumulates and RuBP decreases—a classic exam question). Understanding these cause-and-effect relationships demonstrates mastery.

⚠️ Common NEET Mistake:

Students confuse the location of ATP synthesis. Light reactions make ATP in thylakoids. The Calvin cycle uses ATP in the stroma. Some exams ask: "In which part of the chloroplast is NADPH used?" (Answer: stroma, in the Calvin cycle). Know your compartments cold—this detail appears in diagram-label questions.

Cellular Respiration: The Energy Release Process

Cellular respiration breaks down glucose to extract energy. It includes aerobic respiration (requires oxygen) and anaerobic respiration (fermentation, without oxygen). NEET Class 12 emphasizes aerobic respiration, which yields approximately 30–32 ATP per glucose molecule—far more efficient than fermentation's 2 ATP.

Glycolysis (Cytoplasm)

Glycolysis is the first stage and splits one glucose molecule (6 carbons) into two pyruvate molecules (3 carbons each). It occurs in the cytoplasm and is anaerobic—it doesn't require oxygen. The net yield is 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Key detail for NEET: Glycolysis includes ten enzyme-catalyzed steps, but exams focus on the regulatory enzymes: hexokinase (first step, often inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate feedback) and phosphofructokinase or PFK (the committed step of glycolysis, heavily regulated by ATP levels and other allosteric modulators).

A favorite exam question: "If you give a cell glucose but no oxygen, what happens to glycolysis?" (Answer: glycolysis continues, but pyruvate is converted to lactate or ethanol to regenerate NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to proceed). This shows understanding of NAD⁺ recycling, which is essential for exam success.

Citric Acid Cycle (Mitochondrial Matrix)

Also called the Krebs cycle or TCA cycle, this occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. Pyruvate is first converted to Acetyl-CoA (losing one CO₂ and one NADH). Then Acetyl-CoA enters the cycle. Each turn of the cycle produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP (or GTP), and releases 2 CO₂. For one glucose, the cycle turns twice, so total yield from both pyruvates is 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 2 ATP, and 4 CO₂.

NEET loves asking: Which steps release CO₂? (Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase). Which enzyme is rate-limiting? (Isocitrate dehydrogenase, inhibited by high NADH and ATP levels—this is regulation via feedback inhibition). Understanding these details separates 4-mark answers from 2-mark guesses.

Electron Transport Chain and Oxidative Phosphorylation (Inner Mitochondrial Membrane)

NADH and FADH₂ from glycolysis and the citric acid cycle donate electrons to the electron transport chain embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. As electrons move through Complexes I, III, and IV, energy is released to pump protons across the membrane. This creates a proton gradient (chemiosmotic gradient). ATP synthase harnesses this gradient to phosphorylate ADP into ATP. The final electron acceptor is oxygen, which combines with electrons and protons to form water.

Critical for NEET: NADH typically yields ~2.5 ATP (enters at Complex I), while FADH₂ yields ~1.5 ATP (enters at Complex III). This stoichiometry often appears in quantitative questions. Also, understand why oxygen is essential: without it, electrons can't flow, the gradient collapses, and ATP synthesis stops. This is why cells die quickly without oxygen.

Why NEET Tests These Processes Together

The NEET examiner includes both topics in the same paper because they form the foundation of bioenergetics. A single question often asks: "Compare the electron transport chains in photosynthesis and cellular respiration." The answer requires you to explain that photosynthesis's electron transport chain moves electrons from water to NADP⁺ (reducing it), while respiration's chain moves electrons from NADH to oxygen (oxidizing organic molecules). This contrast reveals your conceptual grip.

Another high-frequency question type: "If a plant is kept in complete darkness, what happens to the Calvin cycle?" You must explain that without light reactions, ATP and NADPH production stops, so 3-phosphoglycerate accumulates and RuBP decreases. The cycle slows dramatically. Then, when light returns, RuBP regenerates quickly, allowing the cycle to resume. This demonstrates understanding of metabolic interdependence—exactly what NEET rewards.

High-Yield Exam Patterns and Question Types

NEET repeats predictable question patterns. First, diagram-labeling questions: You'll see a diagram of a chloroplast or mitochondrion and must label structures (thylakoid, stroma, cristae, matrix, etc.) and identify where specific reactions occur. Missing even one label costs points. Second, mechanism questions: "Explain how the thylakoid membrane generates ATP" or "Why is oxygen essential for the electron transport chain?" These require mechanistic reasoning, not memorization. Third, comparison questions: "Distinguish between cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation" or "Compare substrate-level and oxidative phosphorylation." Fourth, quantitative questions: "How many ATP molecules are produced from one glucose in aerobic respiration?" (Answer: approximately 30–32, accounting for NADH and FADH₂ conversion). Master these patterns, and you'll handle most variants.

One more pattern: regulatory and inhibition questions. "Which enzyme in the Calvin cycle is rate-limiting and why?" (Rubisco due to low Km and high Km for CO₂). "How does high ATP concentration inhibit glycolysis?" (Allosteric inhibition of PFK). These demonstrate deep understanding and earn full marks on 3-4 mark questions.

💡 Topper's Insight:

Always link energy molecules: ATP is a temporary energy carrier (used immediately), while glucose is long-term storage. NADH and FADH₂ are electron carriers that act as intermediaries. When an exam asks "why is ATP not used for long-term energy storage in cells," answer: because ATP is expensive to make and hydrolyzes quickly (half-life of seconds). Glucose, however, is stable and energy-dense. This reasoning appears in 2-mark questions but distinguishes toppers from average scorers.

Your next step is deliberate practice. Work through NCERT diagrams until you can redraw them from memory. Solve at least 10 numerical questions on ATP yield from different starting substrates (1 glucose, 1 pyruvate, 1 acetyl-CoA). Use flashcards to memorize enzyme names, their locations, and their regulatory mechanisms. Most importantly, understand the why behind every process—not just the what. When you can explain how a chloroplast and a mitochondrion are both energy-conversion machines, just in opposite directions, you're ready for NEET. Good luck.
