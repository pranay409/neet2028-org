Thermodynamics for NEET: Laws, Processes and Exam Shortcuts

By Karan Malhotra · 📅 November 06, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Thermodynamics is one of the most formula-heavy yet conceptually rich chapters in NEET Physics. From NCERT Chapter 12 (Thermodynamics), you'll face 2-3 questions per exam, worth 8-12 marks. Most students lose points not because they don't know the formulas, but because they misidentify the process or fail to apply the first law correctly in exam pressure. This guide walks you through the exact framework that top scorers use to nail thermodynamics questions—with zero wasted time.

Understanding the Core Framework: The Four Pillars of Thermodynamics

Before memorizing formulas, you need to lock in the foundational mindset. Thermodynamics sits on four pillars: the internal energy concept, the first law, the second law, and reversible vs. irreversible processes. NEET focuses heavily on the first law application across different processes (isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric, isobaric) because it demands both conceptual clarity and algebraic speed.

Internal energy (U) depends only on temperature for an ideal gas. This single fact eliminates 40% of the confusion in thermodynamics problems. If temperature doesn't change, ΔU = 0. That's it. Yet students often forget this and get trapped in needless calculations.

The first law states: ΔU = Q − W, where Q is heat absorbed and W is work done by the gas. Master this statement in multiple forms because NEET rewrites it constantly. Sometimes it's given as ΔU = Q + W (if W is work done on the gas), and careless sign errors cost full marks.

The Four Processes: Recognition and Quick Calculations

Isothermal Process (T = constant)

In an isothermal process, temperature stays fixed, so ΔU = 0. This means Q = W. The gas absorbs heat and converts it entirely to work. NEET typically asks: "A gas undergoes isothermal expansion. Find heat absorbed." Students panic, but you just need W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = P₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁), and Q = W.

NCERT Chapter 12 emphasizes the PV graph: isothermal curves are hyperbolas. A common exam trap: showing an isothermal expansion and asking for ΔU. Students sometimes write ΔU = nCᵥΔT, forgetting that ΔT = 0. Your answer should be ΔU = 0 immediately, with no calculation required.

Adiabatic Process (Q = 0)

Zero heat exchange means ΔU = −W, or equivalently, the work done by the gas equals the decrease in internal energy. This process is fast—think of rapid gas expansion or compression without time for heat transfer.

The critical adiabatic relations are PVᵞ = constant and TVᵞ⁻¹ = constant, where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ (1.4 for diatomic gases like O₂ and N₂). NEET questions almost always involve finding final pressure or temperature after adiabatic compression/expansion. Use TVᵞ⁻¹ = constant if you know temperatures and volumes; use PVᵞ = constant if you know pressures and volumes.

⚡ Common Mistake Alert:

Students write TVᵞ = constant instead of TVᵞ⁻¹ = constant. Remember: the exponent is (γ−1), not γ. This single error flips your answer completely. Cross-check: for a monatomic gas, γ = 5/3, so γ−1 = 2/3. Test this against any known adiabatic problem to build confidence.

Isochoric (Constant Volume) Process

Volume is fixed, so W = 0. The first law becomes ΔU = Q. All heat absorbed increases internal energy and temperature. These are the simplest problems numerically but students often misread the question and assume isobaric (constant pressure) instead.

Key insight: In a PV diagram, isochoric processes are vertical lines. If the question shows a vertical line, immediately recognize that work done is zero.

Isobaric (Constant Pressure) Process

Pressure is constant, so W = PΔV = nRΔT. The first law is ΔU = nCᵥΔT + nRΔT = nCₚΔT. Heat absorbed is Q = nCₚΔT. These are common in NEET because they bridge all three quantities (heat, work, internal energy change).

NCERT Chapter 11 and 12 teach that Cₚ = Cᵥ + R, a relation you must memorize and use reflexively. For diatomic gases: Cᵥ = (5/2)R and Cₚ = (7/2)R.

Exam-Specific Shortcut: The NEET Thermodynamics Checklist

When you see a thermodynamics problem, run through this checklist in 10 seconds. It identifies the process, tells you which formula applies, and prevents careless errors:

  1. Identify the process: Is T, V, P, or Q held constant? Or is it cyclic?
  2. Find ΔU: Always calculate this first. ΔU = nCᵥΔT. If ΔT = 0, then ΔU = 0 (isothermal). If nothing changes, ΔU = 0 (cyclic).
  3. Find W: Isothermal → W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁). Adiabatic → use TVᵞ⁻¹ to find final T, then ΔU = −W. Isochoric → W = 0. Isobaric → W = nRΔT.
  4. Find Q: Use Q = ΔU + W (always true). Never try to memorize Q for each process; derive it from the first law.

This method eliminates confusion because you're not juggling four different "rules"—you're applying one universal rule (ΔU = Q − W) with one universal calculation for ΔU (nCᵥΔT).
