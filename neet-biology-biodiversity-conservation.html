Biodiversity and Conservation for NEET: Key Terms and Exam Patterns

By Karan Malhotra · 📅 October 11, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Biology

Biodiversity and Conservation (NCERT Biology Class 12, Chapter 15) consistently accounts for 2-3 questions on the NEET exam, worth 6-9 marks. Most students treat this chapter as theory-heavy and memory-intensive, then panic when they realize the NEET examiners ask specifics about hotspots, conservation strategies, and biodiversity loss mechanisms—not just definitions. If you're scoring below 70% in this chapter, it's likely because you're memorizing definitions instead of understanding patterns. This guide breaks down exactly what the NEET exam wants from you and how to study it like a topper.

Understanding Biodiversity: Types and Exam Weightage

NEET defines biodiversity as the variety of organisms, genes, and ecosystems. The exam focuses on three key levels, and you'll see 1-2 MCQs testing your understanding of each.

Genetic Diversity

This is variation within a species. NEET often asks about why a crop variety needs genetic diversity or what happens when you use a single hybrid variety across a region. The answer: monoculture leads to vulnerability to pests and disease. Students often confuse this with population diversity—don't. Genetic diversity is about alleles and genes within the same species, and the exam loves asking about agricultural collapse scenarios.

Species Diversity

The number and relative proportion of different species in an area. Here's where the exam gets specific: you need to know the formula for species richness and species evenness. Species richness is simply the count of species; species evenness is how evenly distributed they are. An ecosystem with 100 species where 1 dominates is less diverse than an ecosystem with 100 species evenly distributed. NEET may ask why a rainforest (high evenness) is more stable than a savanna with one dominant grass species.

Ecological Diversity

Variety of habitats and ecosystems. This is the broadest level and the least commonly tested, but it forms the backdrop for understanding why protecting different ecosystem types (forests, wetlands, coral reefs) matters. When NEET asks "why do we need to preserve both grasslands and forests," they're testing ecological diversity awareness.

Biodiversity Hotspots: Memorize These 8

The exam loves hotspot questions. You'll see either "name a biodiversity hotspot" or "identify which region is a hotspot based on these characteristics." NEET has specifically asked about the Western Ghats, Himalayas, and Indo-Burma region in recent years.

The 8 Global Hotspots (NEET Focus)

  • Western Ghats (India) – 4% of world's species, high endemism, tropical deciduous forest. Know: Why it's threatened (human settlement, coffee plantations).
  • Himalayas (India) – Alpine and temperate diversity, restricted ranges due to altitude. Know: Fragmentation threat from roads and hydropower.
  • Indo-Burma – Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia. Lower species richness than Western Ghats but high threat level. NEET rarely goes deep here, but recognize the name.
  • Sundaland – Indonesia, Malaysia. Know it exists; unlikely to be tested in depth.
  • Philippines – Island endemism, fragmented forests.
  • Madagascar – Exceptional endemism (90% of fauna found nowhere else). This is a NEET favorite because it's an extreme example.
  • Brazil's Atlantic Forest – One of the most endangered hotspots; severely deforested.
  • Mesoamerica – Central America and southern Mexico.

For each hotspot, remember: endemism (species found only there), threat level, and the primary cause of biodiversity loss (habitat destruction, hunting, climate change). NEET often pairs hotspot questions with conservation strategy questions—if a region is a hotspot with high endemism, the strategy should prioritize in-situ conservation (protecting it where it grows).

Causes of Biodiversity Loss: The Exam Loves These Mechanisms

This section accounts for 1-2 marks and tests deeper understanding. Students who memorize "habitat loss, overexploitation, pollution" without examples often miss these questions. Here's what actually appears:

Habitat Fragmentation

Not just "habitat loss"—fragmentation is more specific. When a forest is divided by roads or agricultural fields, species that need large ranges (tigers, elephants) can't survive even if total forest area remains the same. NEET has asked: "Why is a 1000 hectare forest divided by a highway less diverse than a connected 1000 hectare forest?" Answer: fragmentation isolates populations, increases inbreeding, reduces gene flow. This tests understanding, not memorization.

Overexploitation and Invasive Species

Overexploitation: Think Steller's sea cow (hunted to extinction), tiger poaching, or overfishing. NEET asks about the mechanism—how does removing a keystone species (like sea otters) cascade through the ecosystem? Invasive species: Lantana in Indian forests, water hyacinth in wetlands. Know the pattern: invasive species outcompete natives because they have no natural predators and often have higher reproductive rates. NEET may ask why an invasive species succeeds—the answer involves lack of coevolution with local enemies.

Pollution and Climate Change

Pollution reduces genetic diversity (DDT caused eagle eggshell thinning; heavy metals accumulate in food chains). Climate change shifts species ranges—tropical species move uphill or poleward, alpine species have nowhere to go. NEET may ask why climate change is a greater threat to endemic species in mountain hotspots.

⚠️ Common Mistake Students Make:

Students memorize "habitat loss, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, climate change" but can't explain mechanisms. When NEET asks "why does habitat loss reduce genetic diversity," they don't say "smaller populations have higher inbreeding and lower effective population size"—instead they say "fewer organisms." That's 0 marks. Always pair causes with mechanisms: fragmentation → isolation → inbreeding, invasive species → competitive exclusion → local extinction.

Conservation Strategies: In-Situ vs. Ex-Situ (Critical for 2-3 Marks)

This is where NEET tests critical thinking. Most students know the definitions but can't apply them to scenarios. Here's the distinction:

In-Situ Conservation

Protecting species in their natural habitat through Protected Areas (PAs). India has national parks (Serengeti model, top-down protection) and sanctuaries (species-specific, more flexible). NEET asks: Why is in-situ conservation preferred? Because it preserves entire ecosystems, maintains natural selection, and is cost-effective long-term. But it has limits—if the habitat itself is threatened (like Kerala's Western Ghats facing plantation expansion), in-situ alone isn't enough.

Ex-Situ Conservation

Breeding endangered species in captivity (zoos, botanical gardens) or maintaining seed banks. NEET tests understanding of when this is necessary: species on the brink of extinction, habitats completely destroyed, or species requiring intensive care (Arabian oryx, Indian vultures poisoned by diclofenac). Know the limitation: ex-situ populations lose genetic diversity through genetic drift in small captive populations, and reintroduction often fails if habitat isn't restored.

India's Conservation Network

NEET asks about India's framework. Know: Project Tiger (1973), Project Elephant (1991), Biosphere Reserves (combining both in-situ and ex-situ), Ramsar sites (wetlands like Sundarbans). For each, understand the goal—Project Tiger isn't just about tigers; it's about protecting entire ecosystems that tigers depend on. Ramsar sites protect migratory bird habitats and are internationally recognized. NEET may ask why the Sundarbans (a Ramsar site) needs both IUCN and national protection—because it's threatened by climate change, salinity, and human encroachment.

Biodiversity Metrics: Simpson's Diversity Index and Shannon Index

NEET doesn't ask for calculations but tests interpretation. Simpson's Index = 1 - (sum of (proportion of species i)^2). Higher values = higher diversity. Shannon Index is more sensitive to rare species. You don't need to calculate these, but you should know: if an ecosystem has one dominant species and many rare species, Shannon Index will be lower than Simpson's because Shannon weights rare species heavily.

In an exam scenario: "Two ecosystems, A and B, both have 100 species. A has one species at 91% and 99 species at 0.09% each. B has all species at 1% each. Which is more diverse?" Answer: B. The exam tests whether you understand evenness matters as much as richness.
