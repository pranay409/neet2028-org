Oscillations for NEET: SHM Concepts That Show Up Every Year

By Sneha Iyer · 📅 October 16, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Oscillations appears in nearly every NEET exam—usually 2-3 questions worth 6-9 marks. Yet students routinely lose 4-5 of those marks because they confuse amplitude with energy, misapply the phase formula, or forget the relationship between spring constant and frequency. The good news: the topics that repeat every year follow a predictable pattern. Once you know what NEET examiners actually test, your score on this chapter jumps from 40% to 85% almost immediately. This guide breaks down exactly which SHM concepts show up, how they're tested, and the single mistake that costs more students marks than any other.

The NEET Oscillations Weightage: What Actually Gets Tested

Chapter 14 of NCERT Physics (Oscillations) is typically worth 6-9 marks, split into 2-3 questions. Here's the exam pattern from the last five years:

  • 1 conceptual MCQ on SHM definitions or phase relationships — Usually asks about amplitude, period, or the difference between frequency and angular frequency
  • 1 calculation-heavy MCQ — Often involves finding maximum velocity, acceleration, or energy in a spring-mass system
  • 1 matching/assertion-reasoning question — Tests whether you understand why certain properties of SHM remain constant or change

The key insight: NEET never asks pure formula memorization. Every question has a conceptual twist. You need to know not just what the formulas are, but when they apply and why.

The Five High-Yield SHM Formulas You Must Master

Rather than memorize 20 formulas, focus on these five. Every other formula in the chapter is derived from these, so if you own these completely, you can solve any oscillation problem on test day.

1. The Defining Equation: x = A sin(ωt + φ)

This is the foundation. x is displacement, A is amplitude (maximum displacement from equilibrium), ω is angular frequency (in rad/s), t is time, and φ is the initial phase. The phase angle (ωt + φ) tells you where in the cycle the oscillator is at any moment. NEET questions frequently ask: "If a particle starts from the mean position, what is its phase at t = 0?" Answer: φ = 0 if moving in the positive direction, φ = π if moving negative.

2. Velocity and Acceleration

Velocity: v = Aω cos(ωt + φ). Maximum velocity is v_max = Aω. Acceleration: a = −ω²x or a = −Aω² sin(ωt + φ). Maximum acceleration is a_max = Aω². Notice: velocity and acceleration are 90° out of phase with displacement. This is tested constantly—if displacement is maximum, velocity is zero, and vice versa.

3. Period and Frequency Relationships

T = 2π/ω and f = 1/T = ω/2π. For a spring: ω = √(k/m), so T = 2π√(m/k). For a simple pendulum: ω = √(g/L), so T = 2π√(L/g). The period depends only on the properties of the system (mass, spring constant, length), not on amplitude or initial conditions. Students often incorrectly think a bigger amplitude means a longer period—it doesn't.

4. Energy Conservation

Total mechanical energy: E = ½kA² = ½mω²A². At any point, this energy splits between kinetic and potential: E = ½mv² + ½kx². At maximum displacement, all energy is potential (v = 0). At equilibrium, all energy is kinetic (x = 0). NEET asks: "A mass oscillating on a spring has amplitude 5 cm. If amplitude doubles, how does total energy change?" Answer: Energy increases by a factor of 4 because E ∝ A².

5. The Phase Difference Between Quantities

Displacement, velocity, and acceleration are related by phase shifts of 90°. If displacement is x = A sin(ωt), then velocity is v = Aω sin(ωt + π/2) and acceleration is a = Aω² sin(ωt + π). This phase relationship is tested in assertion-reasoning questions: "Assertion: When displacement is maximum, acceleration is maximum. Reason: Both are directly proportional to amplitude."—The assertion is false; the reason is irrelevant because acceleration is maximum when displacement is zero.

⚠️ Common Exam Mistake: Confusing Maximum Velocity with Maximum Acceleration

Students often think v_max and a_max occur at the same time. They don't. v_max = Aω occurs when the particle crosses equilibrium (x = 0), while a_max = Aω² occurs at maximum displacement (x = A). Missing this cost an estimated 15% of NEET takers 1-2 marks in 2024.

The Question Patterns That Repeat Every Year

Pattern 1: Spring-Mass Systems with Energy Questions

"A 2 kg mass on a spring (k = 50 N/m) oscillates with amplitude 10 cm. Find the maximum velocity." Students must connect T = 2π√(m/k), then ω = 2π/T, then v_max = Aω. The mistake: treating k like it's given in the problem, not calculating it from the system. Solution: ω = √(50/2) = 5 rad/s, so v_max = 0.1 × 5 = 0.5 m/s.

Pattern 2: Pendulum Period Comparisons

"Two pendulums have lengths L and 4L. Their period ratio is..." Students who remember T ∝ √L immediately get T₂/T₁ = √(4L/L) = 2. Those who confused T with T² or forgot the square root lose the mark.

Pattern 3: Displacement-Velocity Phase Recognition

NEET shows a graph of displacement vs. time and asks which graph represents velocity. Students must recognize that velocity peaks when displacement crosses zero and is zero when displacement is maximum. A student who doesn't understand the 90° phase shift between x and v chooses wrong immediately.

Pattern 4: Changing Amplitude or Initial Conditions

"A mass oscillates with amplitude 5 cm. If you double the initial velocity, what is the new amplitude?" Students must use energy: E = ½mω²A². If initial KE doubles, and this becomes the total energy, then A² quadruples, so A_new = A√4 = 10 cm (not 10 cm, because you're not doubling amplitude—you're doubling the energy that converts to amplitude).

Why SHM Equations Feel Confusing (And How to Fix It)

Most NEET students struggle with oscillations because the formulas feel abstract—there's no obvious real-world connection. Here's the reframe: SHM is just circular motion projected onto a line. Imagine a particle moving in a circle at constant angular velocity ω. Now project its position onto a vertical axis. That projection is SHM. The radius of the circle is the amplitude A. The angle swept out is the phase (ωt + φ). The speed in the circle is Aω. When the particle is at the side of the circle, its vertical component is maximum (v_max), but its vertical displacement is zero. When it's at the top or bottom of the circle, vertical displacement is maximum (±A), but vertical velocity is zero.

Once you visualize SHM this way, every relationship makes sense. The 90° phase shift between x and v? That's because the perpendicular direction to velocity is displacement. The reason period is independent of amplitude? Because the particle always travels the same circular path, just with different radii—it takes the same time to complete one circle regardless of radius size (in uniform circular motion). NEET examiners test your intuition through this lens, not just formula plugging.
