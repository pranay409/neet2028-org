S-Block Elements for NEET: Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

By Divya Reddy · 📅 November 26, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

S-block elements (Groups 1 and 2) consistently deliver 15–20 marks in NEET, yet many students mishandle this chapter because they memorize without understanding periodicity. This isn't just about knowing that sodium is more reactive than lithium—it's about predicting why, explaining the chemistry behind anomalies, and solving real-world application problems under exam pressure. If you skip the "why" and memorize only the "what," you'll lose points on comparison questions, hydration energy problems, and anomalies you've never seen before. This guide walks you through the exact framework NEET toppers use to master alkali and alkaline earth metals.

Understanding S-Block Structure and Electronic Configuration

S-block elements occupy Groups 1 and 2 of the periodic table, with their valence electrons in s-orbitals. Group 1 (alkali metals: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) has the configuration [Noble gas]ns¹, while Group 2 (alkaline earth metals: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) has [Noble gas]ns². This fundamental difference in valence electrons determines everything—reactivity, ionization energy, hydration behavior, and compound formation.

NCERT Chapter 10 (The S-Block Elements) is your primary source, but most students rush through the electronic configuration section and miss the connection to chemical properties. The key insight: as you move down a group, the valence electron gets farther from the nucleus, shielding increases, and despite increasing nuclear charge, it becomes easier to remove that electron. This is why cesium is more reactive than lithium, not less—a counterintuitive fact that trip up careless readers.

Many NEET questions test your ability to predict trends without being given data. For example, "Which of the following has the highest first ionization energy in Group 1?" The answer is lithium, not because Li is the smallest (it is), but because the valence electron experiences the highest effective nuclear charge relative to its distance. Understanding this reasoning helps you tackle unfamiliar questions confidently.

Alkali Metals: Properties, Reactions, and Exam Patterns

Physical and Chemical Properties

Alkali metals are soft, silvery-white metals with low densities and low melting points. The critical trends to memorize and explain are:

  • Atomic radius increases down the group (Li → Cs): Each new period adds an electron shell, pushing the valence electron farther out.
  • First ionization energy decreases down the group: Lower effective nuclear charge on the outermost electron makes it easier to remove.
  • Reactivity increases down the group: Lower ionization energy means alkali metals lose electrons more readily, making them more reactive.
  • Density increases irregularly: Potassium is less dense than sodium (an anomaly), breaking the expected trend—this is a frequent NEET trick question.
  • Melting and boiling points decrease down the group: Metallic bonding weakens as atomic size increases.

NEET often asks comparative questions like "Which alkali metal has the lowest density?" (Lithium) or "Arrange Na, K, Rb in order of reactivity" (Rb > K > Na). These aren't just memorization—they test whether you've grasped the periodic trend concept. If you understand why reactivity increases, you can predict the order without memorizing.

Chemical Reactions of Alkali Metals

NEET questions on alkali metals predominantly focus on their reactions with common substances. Expect 2–3 reaction-based questions per exam:

  • With oxygen: Alkali metals burn in oxygen to form oxides, peroxides, or superoxides depending on the metal. Lithium forms primarily Li₂O (oxide), sodium forms Na₂O₂ (peroxide), and potassium onwards form KO₂ (superoxide). This trend is critical: the larger the metal, the more "room" there is in the peroxide/superoxide structure to stabilize it. Many students confuse these products—make sure you can write balanced equations for each.
  • With water: Alkali metals react vigorously with water, releasing H₂ gas and forming strong alkaline solutions (MOH). The reaction becomes more violent down the group: lithium reacts gently, sodium burns with orange flame, potassium catches fire spontaneously. NEET rarely asks you to describe the flame color without context, but it often asks which metal reacts most/least vigorously or what products form.
  • With hydrogen: Alkali metals form ionic hydrides (MH) with hydrogen. These hydrides are strong bases and reduce H⁺ ions to H₂ gas when added to water.
  • With halogens: Alkali metals form ionic halides (MX) with halogens. The reactivity order Na > Li for a given halogen surprises many students—lithium's small size leads to higher lattice energy and different reactivity patterns in some contexts.

Anomalous Behavior of Lithium

Lithium is the most anomalous alkali metal, and NEET loves testing this. Unlike other Group 1 metals, lithium:

  • Has the highest melting point in the group (not lowest like others).
  • Has the highest density in the group.
  • Forms primarily oxides, not peroxides (like heavier alkali metals).
  • Forms covalent compounds, not purely ionic ones (LiCl is partly covalent).
  • Has high charge density and hence high hydration energy.

These anomalies stem from lithium's small size and high charge density. Its compounds often resemble magnesium compounds (diagonal relationship), which NEET uses for comparison questions: "Which of the following statements is true about lithium?" often includes options about its similarity to magnesium.

Alkaline Earth Metals: Properties and Distinctive Chemistry

Overview and Trends

Alkaline earth metals (Group 2) show similar trends to alkali metals but with important differences due to their +2 oxidation state:

  • Atomic radius increases down the group (Be → Ra), following the periodic trend.
  • First ionization energy decreases down the group, but all alkaline earth metals have higher ionization energies than their Group 1 counterparts.
  • Reactivity increases down the group, but alkaline earth metals are generally less reactive than alkali metals because removing two electrons requires more energy.
  • Solubility of hydroxides increases down the group: Be(OH)₂ is least soluble, Ba(OH)₂ is most soluble. This is a frequent source of confusion and NEET questions.
  • Solubility of sulfates decreases down the group: BeSO₄ is soluble, while BaSO₄ is nearly insoluble. This reversal of trends is examined often.

Chemical Reactions and Industrial Significance

Alkaline earth metals are less reactive than alkali metals but still react with oxygen, water, and other non-metals. Key reactions include:

  • With oxygen: Mostly form oxides (MgO, CaO). Barium can form peroxide (BaO₂) under special conditions.
  • With water: React to form hydroxides and H₂, but less vigorously than alkali metals. Be and Mg react only with hot water or steam; Ca, Sr, Ba react with cold water.
  • With acids: Form salts and release H₂ gas. Magnesium's reaction with dilute acids is used in the lab to generate hydrogen gas.

NEET often includes application-based questions: "Quicklime (CaO) is used in construction because..." (it's a strong base and hydrates to form Ca(OH)₂). "Barium sulfate is used as a contrast agent in medical imaging because..." (it's insoluble and opaque to X-rays). These application questions distinguish high scorers from average ones.

Anomalous Behavior of Beryllium

Like lithium, beryllium exhibits anomalous properties within its group:

  • Be(OH)₂ is amphoteric, dissolving in both acids and strong bases (unlike other Group 2 hydroxides which are basic).
  • BeO has very high melting point due to its small size and high charge density.
  • Beryllium compounds are often covalent, not purely ionic.
  • Beryllium shows diagonal relationship with aluminum (Be²⁺ and Al³⁺ have similar charge densities).

NEET questions often pair beryllium anomalies with lithium anomalies in comparison questions, testing whether you understand charge density as the underlying cause.
