P-Block Elements for NEET: Patterns, Properties and Predictions

By Priya Nair · 📅 November 07, 2026 · ⏱ 11 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

P-block elements—groups 13 through 18 of the periodic table—constitute one of the most heavily tested chapters in NEET Chemistry. With approximately 4–6 questions appearing every year, understanding the patterns, properties, and reactions of p-block elements is non-negotiable if you want a strong Chemistry score. What makes this topic particularly challenging is that it demands both conceptual clarity and factual recall: you need to know why boron doesn't follow the octet rule, how fluorine's electronegativity shapes reactivity, and which allotropes of phosphorus are most commonly asked about in MCQs.

The real pressure comes from the breadth. NCERT Chapter 11 (The p-Block Elements) covers groups 13 through 18 with significant depth—from trends in oxidation states to anomalies in the first member of each group. Many students approach this by memorising reactions, which leads to confusion and lost marks. Instead, if you understand the underlying periodic trends and use the right framework, you can predict properties and reactions you've never seen before, making you unstoppable on exam day.

The Periodic Trend Framework: Why It Matters for Predictions

The key to mastering p-block elements is understanding that nearly every property and reaction follows predictable periodic trends. These trends—electronegativity, atomic radius, ionisation energy, and oxidation states—determine how elements behave.

Electronegativity and Reactivity Patterns

Electronegativity increases as you move right across a period (boron to fluorine) and decreases down a group (fluorine to iodine). This single fact explains almost everything: why fluorine is the strongest oxidising agent, why iodine is the weakest halogen, and why carbon forms stable covalent compounds while lead tends toward ionic behaviour.

In NEET, questions often test your ability to predict which halogen will displace another in solution. For example, chlorine displaces both bromine and iodine because chlorine is more electronegative and a stronger oxidising agent. But iodine cannot displace chlorine. This isn't random—it flows directly from the electronegativity trend. When you see a displacement question, don't memorise the answer; reason through the electronegativity values and you'll always get it right.

The Anomaly of the First Member

Group 13 starts with boron, a non-metal. Group 14 starts with carbon, also fundamentally different from silicon below it. Fluorine in group 17 has properties that set it apart from chlorine and beyond. These aren't exceptions to learn separately—they're consequences of the first member's small size and high electronegativity.

Boron's small size means it can't expand its octet, so it forms only three covalent bonds and acts as a Lewis acid. This explains why boron trifluoride (BF₃) readily accepts electron pairs—a fact tested in resonance and molecular geometry questions. Carbon, being the smallest group 14 element, forms strong double and triple bonds, enabling vast organic chemistry. Fluorine's exceptional electronegativity makes it the only element that can reduce water and form compounds in negative oxidation states—questions testing your understanding of F₂'s reaction with water are common in NEET.

Groups 13–15: Boron, Aluminum, Gallium and Beyond

In groups 13 and 14, the oxidation state pattern shifts. Boron and carbon show predominantly +3 and +4 oxidation states (or covalent bonding with these formal charges). But as you move down—to silicon, germanium, tin, and lead—the +2 oxidation state becomes increasingly stable. This trend is called the "inert pair effect" and is absolutely crucial for NEET.

Lead, at the bottom of group 14, actually shows +2 as its most common oxidation state because the outermost s electrons become harder to remove. NEET examiners love testing this: they'll ask whether Pb²⁺ or Pb⁴⁺ is more stable (Pb²⁺ is), or which is a stronger reducing agent (Pb²⁺). If you understand the inert pair effect, you'll reason through these instantly.

Boron compounds like borax and boric acid deserve specific attention. These hydrated borates form intricate polymeric structures and are mild Lewis acids—borax, Na₂B₄O₅(OH)₄·8H₂O, hydrolyses to produce boric acid. NEET has tested whether borax solution is acidic or basic; if you know boric acid is a weak monobasic acid, you'll know borax is weakly basic. Similarly, aluminium hydroxide Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric—it dissolves in both strong acids and strong bases—a behaviour that appears in nearly every NEET paper's inorganic chemistry section.

Groups 16 and 17: Oxygen, Sulfur, Fluorine and Halogens—The High-Frequency Exam Topics

Groups 16 and 17 appear in more questions per unit area than any other part of the p-block. Oxygen and sulfur chemistry is foundational (sulfuric acid, sulfites, thiosulfates), while halogen chemistry directly tests your grasp of oxidation states, redox reactions, and disproportionation.

Oxygen and Sulfur: Reactivity and Oxidation State Stability

Oxygen, being highly electronegative and small, almost never shows positive oxidation states—except in OF₂ and O₂F₂, compounds where fluorine is even more electronegative. Sulfur, below oxygen, is larger and more polarisable, so it can expand its octet and show +4 and +6 oxidation states in compounds like SO₂ and H₂SO₄.

A classic NEET question tests whether a sulfur atom in H₂SO₄ is oxidised or reduced when it reacts with a reducing agent. The answer hinges on recognising that sulfur in +6 can only be reduced, never oxidised further. This is why concentrated sulfuric acid acts as an oxidising agent—the sulfur wants to drop from +6 to a lower state.

Halogens: Disproportionation and Oxidation State Trends
