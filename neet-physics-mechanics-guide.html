NEET Physics Mechanics: 15 Concepts You Cannot Afford to Miss

By Aditya Joshi · 📅 October 28, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Mechanics carries approximately 90–100 marks in NEET (out of 720 total), spread across NCERT Physics Classes 11 and 12. That's roughly 13% of your entire score. Yet most students treat it as a "standard" topic and skip the depth. The reality? Mechanics questions in NEET are designed to separate scorers from scorers. If you don't grip these 15 core concepts—with their nuances, edge cases, and problem-solving hacks—you'll lose 15–20 marks that could have been the difference between 600 and 650, or between a top medical college and a waitlist.

This guide walks you through the mechanics landscape as a NEET topper would explain it to a struggling classmate. No fluff. Just the concepts that appear, the mistakes students make, and the exam patterns you need to recognize instantly.

1. Newton's Laws & Free Body Diagrams: The Foundation That Breaks Scores

Newton's three laws (NCERT Class 11, Chapter 5) form the skeleton of every mechanics problem. Yet 60% of students draw FBDs incorrectly. You must identify every force: weight, normal reaction, friction, tension, applied force. The most common error? Forgetting pseudo forces in non-inertial frames, which NEET loves. When the ground accelerates, add a pseudo force on all objects relative to that frame.

Exam pattern: NEET tests this through 2–3 questions per year. One is usually conceptual (identifying forces correctly), another involves an accelerating reference frame. Practice drawing FBDs until it becomes second nature, because every complex mechanics problem starts here.

2. Friction: Static vs Kinetic, and the Threshold Students Miss

NCERT Chapter 5 (Class 11) covers friction minimally, but NEET examiners probe the difference relentlessly. Static friction adapts up to μₛN, kinetic is always μₖN. Most students apply kinetic friction too early. A block on a table being gently pushed will not slide until the applied force exceeds μₛN—it sticks, static friction balances the push. Only when you exceed that threshold does kinetic friction kick in.

In inclined plane problems, students often assume the block slides down. Check: is mg sin θ > μₛ mg cos θ? If no, the block stays put and static friction equals mg sin θ. This distinction appears in 1–2 NEET questions annually and trips up half the candidates.

3. Circular Motion: Centripetal Force is Not Real (But Its Effect Is)

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 4 introduces circular motion, and NEET embeds questions in conical pendulums, banked tracks, and rotating cylinders. The critical concept: centripetal force is not a new force. It's the net inward force you calculate from existing forces. A stone on a string experiences tension, which provides centripetal acceleration. The string doesn't "pull the stone inward"—the tension does.

Common exam trap: "What happens when the string breaks?" Students panic and say "centripetal force disappears, object flies off." Correct answer: the net force becomes zero (or becomes gravity/friction), so the object moves in a straight line tangent to its circular path. The centripetal force never "held it in"—it was just tension pointing inward. Once tension is gone, inward direction is meaningless.

4. Work, Energy & Power: The Energy Theorem Saves Time

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 6. Most students waste time setting up kinematic equations when work-energy theorem solves it in one line: W_net = ΔKE. If a car accelerates from 20 m/s to 30 m/s over 100 m, don't find acceleration first. Net work equals change in kinetic energy. This is elegant and matches NEET's pace.

Watch for conservative vs non-conservative forces. Gravity and spring are conservative (potential energy exists). Friction is non-conservative (energy dissipates as heat). A ball thrown upward loses kinetic energy; without friction, all of it converts to gravitational potential energy. With air resistance, some is lost to heat—mechanical energy decreases but total energy (including heat) is conserved.

NEET tests this with questions like: "A block slides down a rough incline. Find the speed at the bottom." Using energy: mgΔh - friction work = ½mv². No need for FBD and kinematics together.

5. Center of Mass & Momentum: The Intellectual Shortcut

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 7. The center of mass moves as if all external forces act on it. For a system with no external forces, momentum is conserved. This shifts the problem's complexity. Instead of tracking each object, track their center of mass. In collisions, even if objects deform, momentum is conserved instantaneously.

Exam trap: A man jumps off a stationary boat. Students ask, "Doesn't the boat move?" Yes—momentum conservation requires it. If the man was 60 kg and the boat 120 kg, and the man jumps at 4 m/s, the boat recoils at 2 m/s backward. Total momentum before = 0 = total momentum after. NEET tests this in 1–2 questions per year, and students who skip the chapter lose them instantly.

6. Collisions: Elastic vs Inelastic, and the Coefficient of Restitution

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 7. Elastic collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy. Inelastic collisions conserve momentum only; kinetic energy is lost. The coefficient of restitution (e) quantifies bounce: e = (relative velocity of separation) / (relative velocity of approach). For elastic collisions, e = 1. For perfectly inelastic, e = 0 (objects stick). NEET loves intermediate values (0 < e < 1).

A ball falling from height h and bouncing to height h': e = √(h'/h). If it bounces to 64% of original height, e = 0.8. This links drops, bounces, and restitution in one elegant equation. Students often memorize collision formulas but forget the e approach—the e approach scales instantly to any collision, not just head-on.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Forgetting Reference Frames

A ball is dropped in a moving train. Does it accelerate horizontally relative to the ground? Yes. Relative to the train? No—it falls straight down. NEET will ask "relative to the platform" or "relative to the train" and flip your answer if you confuse frames. Always identify the reference frame explicitly before solving.

7. Rotational Motion: Torque & Angular Momentum's Elegant Analogy

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 7 (briefly) and Class 12, Chapter 10. Rotation mirrors translation: force ↔ torque, mass ↔ moment of inertia, acceleration ↔ angular acceleration, momentum ↔ angular momentum. τ = Iα, just as F = ma. Angular momentum L = Iω is conserved if no external torque acts. A spinning skater pulls arms in: I decreases, so ω increases (speeds up). Energy? ½Iω² increases—where does it come from? The skater does work pulling in their arms against the centrifugal effect.

NEET tests this in rolling motion (Chapter 7, Class 11): a cylinder rolling down an incline is faster than sliding because gravitational potential energy splits between translational KE and rotational KE. Using energy conservation: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω². For a solid cylinder, I = ½mr², and ω = v/r, so mgh = ½mv² + ½(½mr²)(v/r)² = ¾mv². Solve for v directly without force analysis. This is the kind of elegant shortcut NEET rewards.

8. Gravitation: Orbital Mechanics & Escape Velocity

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 8. Gravitational force provides centripetal force for orbital motion: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so v = √(GM/r). Escape velocity is the speed needed to reach infinity with zero velocity: ½mv_e² = GMm/r, so v_e = √(2GM/r). Notice v_e = √2 × v_orbital. NEET often compares these: if a planet's escape velocity is 12 km/s, orbital velocity at the surface is 12/√2 ≈ 8.5 km/s.

A satellite's total energy is E = -GMm/(2r). It's negative (bound system). Kinetic energy is positive, potential is negative, and |PE| = 2×KE (virial theorem). If a satellite orbits at radius r and burns fuel to reduce its orbit to r/2, its speed increases (seems paradoxical but gravitational binding is tighter), and it loses total energy (fuel was ejected, taking energy away). This appears in 1–2 NEET questions annually.

9. Simple Harmonic Motion: Amplitude, Phase & Energy

NCERT Class 11, Chapter 13. SHM is motion where acceleration is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction: a = -ω²x. The general solution is x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ). NEET tests recognition of SHM (does a = -kx?), finding amplitude A and angular frequency ω
