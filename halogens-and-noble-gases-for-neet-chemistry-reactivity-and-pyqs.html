Halogens and Noble Gases for NEET: Chemistry, Reactivity and PYQs

By Aditya Joshi · 📅 November 27, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

Halogens and noble gases are two of the most chemistry-heavy topics in NEET's p-block unit, and they represent opposite extremes of chemical behavior. While halogens appear in 3–4 questions per exam (often worth 8–12 marks), noble gases seem inert but require deep understanding of their occasional compounds and oxidation states. If you've struggled with why fluorine doesn't follow typical halogen trends, or why xenon can form compounds when helium cannot, this guide walks you through every concept, every common pitfall, and every PYQ pattern you need to master.

Halogens: Properties, Reactivity Trends, and NEET Patterns

Halogens occupy Group 17 of the periodic table (NCERT Chapter 10: The s-block Elements, p-block Elements). They possess seven valence electrons and exist in diverse physical states at room temperature—fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is liquid, and iodine is a solid. This variation alone is a frequent NEET question stem: students are asked to explain why iodine is solid while chlorine is gas, requiring knowledge of van der Waals forces and atomic size.

Atomic and Ionic Radii Trends

As you move down Group 17, atomic radii increase due to addition of electron shells: F < Cl < Br < I. This trend is fundamental because it explains why F₂ is the smallest diatomic halogen, exhibits the highest bond dissociation energy (~159 kcal/mol), yet paradoxically has lower electronegativity trends in some reactions compared to chlorine. Many students confuse electronegativity with reactivity here—the key insight is that fluorine's small size makes F-F repulsion significant, so its bond is not as strong as Cl-Cl. This is why fluorine is more reactive than chlorine as an oxidizing agent in aqueous solution, but F₂ gas can be less reactive than Cl₂ in certain solid-state reactions.

Oxidation States and Oxoacids

Halogens exhibit multiple oxidation states: -1, 0, +1, +3, +5, and +7. This variety makes halogen chemistry a favorite NEET topic. The oxoacids of chlorine illustrate this perfectly: HClO (hypochlorous acid, Cl: +1), HClO₂ (chlorous acid, Cl: +3), HClO₃ (chloric acid, Cl: +5), and HClO₄ (perchloric acid, Cl: +7). The acidity order is HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄, explained by the number of oxygen atoms bonded to the central halogen—more oxygens withdraw electron density, increasing acidity. NEET frequently asks students to rank these acids or predict which is the strongest oxidizing agent (answer: HClO₄, because Cl is in its highest oxidation state and wants to be reduced).

⚠️ Common Mistake: Confusing Reactivity with Bond Strength

Students often say "fluorine is most reactive so F₂ has the strongest bond," but this is wrong. F₂ bond strength (~159 kcal/mol) is lower than Cl₂ (~243 kcal/mol) due to F-F electron repulsion in the small orbital space. Fluorine's high reactivity stems from its high electronegativity and the resulting large negative charge on the fluoride ion. Always separate bond strength from reactivity tendency—a question asking "which halogen requires most energy to break the diatomic molecule?" expects Cl₂, not F₂.

Halogen Chemistry: Displacement Reactions and Disproportionation

NEET p-block questions often test displacement series. Because F is most electronegative, F₂ displaces all other halogens: F₂ + 2Cl⁻ → 2F⁻ + Cl₂, and so on down to I⁻. This is a classic 1-mark filler but can appear in complex equilibrium contexts. More importantly, halogens undergo disproportionation in aqueous or alkaline solution: Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO (in cold dilute solution). In hot concentrated NaOH, disproportionation proceeds further: 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O. NEET has asked students to identify what Cl₂ forms under different conditions—if you memorize only one reaction, your answer will be wrong. The key is recognizing that in cold, dilute, neutral conditions, you get a mixture of chloride and hypochlorite; in hot, concentrated basic conditions, you get chlorate.

Interhalogen Compounds

When two different halogens react, they form interhalogen compounds like ClF, ClF₃, ClF₅, BrF₃, IF₅, and IF₇. These are valuable NEET topics because they test both structure and bonding. The general formula is X Y_n, where X is the larger (less electronegative) halogen and Y is fluorine (the most electronegative). Many students struggle with VSEPR geometries here: ClF₃ has a T-shaped structure (not trigonal planar), and IF₇ has a pentagonal bipyramidal structure. NEET diagrams often ask students to predict the shape or identify which is linear (answer: only ClF and BrF are linear among the simple interhalogen compounds).

Noble Gases: Inert to Reactive—The Xenon Compounds

Noble gases (Group 18, NCERT Chapter 10) were long thought to be absolutely inert. However, in 1962, Bartlett discovered XePtF₆, revolutionizing inorganic chemistry. NEET now regularly asks students why He and Ne form no compounds, while Xe forms stable compounds like XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆, XeO₃, and XeO₄²⁻. The answer lies in atomic size and ionization energy: Xe is large enough and its ionization energy low enough that it can release electrons to highly electronegative atoms like fluorine or oxygen.

Xenon Fluorides: Structure and Reactivity

Xenon fluorides are the gold standard of NEET p-block questions. XeF₂ has a linear structure (3 lone pairs on Xe in equatorial positions, 2 F atoms in axial positions via VSEPR). XeF₄ is square planar (2 lone pairs equatorial, 4 F atoms in the plane). XeF₆ is a distorted octahedral (1 lone pair causes slight distortion). These structures are frequently shown in NEET diagrams with students asked to identify bond angles or explain the shape. The key strategy: always apply VSEPR theory systematically. Xe has 8 valence electrons; each F contributes 1 for bonding, so for XeF₂ you have 2 bonding pairs + 3 lone pairs = 5 electron pairs, hence trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry but linear molecular geometry.

Xenon Oxides and Oxoacids

XeO₃ and XeO₄ are strong oxidizing agents often tested in redox reactions. XeO₃ is an explosive solid; XeO₄²⁻ is stable in solution. NEET may ask what happens when XeF₆ reacts with water: XeF₆ + 3H₂O → XeO₃ + 6HF. The products (XeO₃ and HF) are themselves significant in later chapters—HF is a weak acid (high H-F bond strength), and XeO₃ can oxidize other species. This interconnectedness is classic NEET design: a question on xenon chemistry might hinge on knowing HF properties from an earlier section.

🎯 Key Insight: Why Only Xe (Rarely Kr) Forms Compounds

Among noble gases, only Xe (and in rare cases Kr) forms stable compounds under normal conditions. Ar and lighter noble gases have ionization energies too high and are too small to accommodate fluorine atoms without extreme conditions. This fact distinguishes strong students from weak ones: if a NEET question asks "which noble gas forms the most stable oxofluoride?", the answer is always Xe. If it asks "can Ne form compounds?", the answer is no under any practical NEET context. Memorize: He, Ne, Ar → no compounds; Kr → very rare compounds; Xe → common, stable compounds.

PYQ Analysis: Common NEET Question Types and Strategies

NEET has asked halogens and noble gases questions in multiple formats. Here are the top patterns with strategies:

  • Reactivity Order Questions: "Which halogen is the strongest oxidizing agent in aqueous solution?" Answer: F₂, because F⁻ has the highest reduction potential. Many students incorrectly choose Cl₂ because it's more reactive in terms of bond breaking—the distinction is crucial. Always remember: aqueous solution means hydration energies matter, and F⁻ is heavily hydrated, making F₂ reduction thermodynamically favorable.
  • Structure and Bonding: "What is the shape of XeF₄?" Expected answer: square planar, with bond angle ~90°. The question often includes a diagram; students must apply VSEPR correctly. Common wrong answer: tetrahedral. The correct reasoning: 4 bonding pairs + 2 lone pairs = 6 electron pairs → octahedral electron geometry → square planar molecular geometry.
  • Disproportionation and Reactions: "Cl₂ in hot concentrated NaOH gives:" Students must know the products are NaCl, NaClO₃, and H₂O, with Cl going to -1 and +5 oxidation states. This requires practice recognizing the specific conditions (hot, concentrated, basic) that lead to this unique stoichiometry.
  • Interhalogen Stability: "Which interhalogen is most stable?" Answer: ClF₃ or ClF (depending on context), because Cl-F bonds are strong and fluorine is the most electronegative. If the question specifies pentavalent interhalogen, the answer is IF₅ or ClF₅ or BrF₅.
  • Oxoacid Acidity: "Arrange HClO, HClO₂, HClO₃, HClO₄ in order of increasing acidity." Answer: HClO < HClO₂ < HClO₃ < HClO₄. The rule: more oxygen atoms → higher acidity. This applies to all oxyacids, so mastering it here aids other chapters.

A crucial exam strategy: when you see a halogen or noble gas question, immediately identify the sub-topic (reactivity, structure, redox, disproportionation) and apply the specific rule. Generic knowledge of "halogens are reactive" will cost you marks; precise, categorized knowledge wins.
