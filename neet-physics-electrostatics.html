Electrostatics for NEET: From Coulomb's Law to Capacitors

By Rohan Verma · 📅 November 03, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Electrostatics appears deceptively simple on paper, but it's one of the highest-scoring yet most misunderstood chapters in NEET Physics. Last year, 8-12 marks came directly from electrostatics concepts (Coulomb's law, electric field, potential, and capacitors combined). The real challenge? Students memorize formulas without understanding the underlying physical intuition. When NEET throws a multi-step numerical or a tricky conceptual question, weak understanding crumbles. This guide breaks down electrostatics from first principles—exactly as a topper would explain it—so you can solve any question with confidence.

Coulomb's Law: The Foundation Everything Rests On

Coulomb's law (NCERT Class 12, Chapter 1) is the bedrock of electrostatics. The law states that the force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance: F = k(q₁q₂)/r². The constant k = 9 × 10⁹ N⋅m²/C² must be memorized and understood, not just plugged in.

Here's where most students slip up: they treat Coulomb's law as a one-dimensional formula and forget the vector nature of force. In reality, forces act along the line joining two charges. When you have three or more charges, you must resolve forces into components and add them vectorially. NEET has repeatedly asked questions where three charges form a triangle and you need to find the net force on one charge—this requires breaking down F into x and y components, then using Pythagoras.

Key Points to Lock In:

  • Force is repulsive for like charges, attractive for opposite charges.
  • The SI unit of charge is Coulomb (C), and 1C is an enormous charge in practice; you'll typically work with μC or nC.
  • Practice at least 5-6 vector force problems where charges are positioned at the corners of a square or equilateral triangle. These appear almost every year in NEET.
  • Know the superposition principle cold: the net force on a charge is the vector sum of individual forces from all other charges.

Electric Field and Potential: The Invisible Architecture

Once you've mastered force, electric field (E) becomes clearer. Electric field is defined as the force per unit charge: E = F/q. It's not a force itself—it's a property of space around a charge. NCERT Chapter 1 introduces this gradually, but many students conflate E and F, leading to errors when calculating potential energy or work done.

The potential (V) is trickier still, and this is where many NEET aspirants lose marks. Potential is the work done per unit charge in bringing a test charge from infinity to a point in the field: V = W/q. Critically, potential is a scalar (unlike field, which is a vector). For a point charge, V = kQ/r. The key insight: equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines, and no work is done moving a charge along an equipotential. NEET loves asking "what's the work done in moving a charge along an equipotential?" The answer is zero—and if you don't nail this conceptually, you'll guess wrong.

Common Exam Patterns in This Section:

  • Calculate the electric field at a point due to multiple point charges (vector addition required).
  • Find the potential at a given location and then the potential difference between two points.
  • Determine work done in moving a charge from point A to B: W = q(V_A − V_B). Remember the sign convention carefully.
  • Identify equipotential surfaces for common charge configurations (point charge, uniform field, etc.).
Common Mistake Alert:

Students often confuse "potential" with "potential energy." Potential energy is U = qV (the product of charge and potential), while potential V is just the field property at a location. When moving a charge q from potential V₁ to V₂, the change in potential energy is ΔU = q(V₂ − V₁), not ΔU = V₂ − V₁. This sign error costs marks repeatedly.

Capacitors: Storing Charge and Energy

Capacitors (NCERT Chapter 2) are where electrostatics transitions from theory to real-world application, and they're a favorite NEET topic. A capacitor is a device that stores electrical charge (and energy) by maintaining a potential difference between two conductors. The capacitance C is defined as C = Q/V, where Q is the charge stored and V is the potential difference.

For a parallel plate capacitor, the capacitance depends on the geometry: C = ε₀εᵣA/d, where A is the area of the plates, d is the separation, ε₀ is the permittivity of free space (8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m), and εᵣ is the relative permittivity of the dielectric material. When you insert a dielectric between the plates, the capacitance increases by a factor of εᵣ. This is a core NEET formula—memorize it and practice variations where the plate area or separation changes.

Energy Storage in Capacitors:

A capacitor stores energy: U = ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/2C. The first formula is easiest to remember for NEET: U = ½CV². When two capacitors are connected in series, the effective capacitance is 1/C_eff = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ (reciprocals add). When in parallel, C_eff = C₁ + C₂. NEET numericals often ask you to find the voltage across each capacitor or the total energy stored in a combination—these require knowing which formula applies and solving step-by-step without rushing.

Another high-value topic: what happens when a dielectric is inserted into a capacitor? If the capacitor remains isolated (not connected to a battery), the charge Q stays constant, but the capacitance increases, so the voltage V decreases. If the capacitor remains connected to the battery, the voltage stays constant, but the charge increases. Toppers distinguish between these two scenarios automatically; struggling students mix them up. Spend time on this distinction with worked examples.

Solving NEET Electrostatics Questions: A Practical Strategy

Every electrostatics question follows a pattern. First, identify what type of problem it is: force calculation, field/potential calculation, or capacitor network. Second, draw a diagram—this prevents silly errors. Third, list what you know and what you need to find. Fourth, select the correct formula or principle. Fifth, solve and check the sign and unit.

In the last 5 years of NEET, electrostatics has contributed 8-12 marks annually, with about 3-4 questions. Typically, one is a straightforward Coulomb's law or capacitor calculation, one involves field and potential concepts, and one combines multiple concepts or requires careful dielectric/energy analysis. By focusing your revision on these three patterns and solving 20-25 high-quality problems from previous NEET papers and NCERT exemplar, you'll be prepared for 90% of what appears in the actual exam.
