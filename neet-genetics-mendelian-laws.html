Mendelian Genetics for NEET: Master the Laws, Ace the Questions

By Arjun Mehta · 📅 October 30, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Biology

Mendelian genetics isn't just another NCERT chapter—it's the foundation of your entire Biology score. Every year, NEET examiners load 8–12 marks on monohybrid crosses, dihybrid crosses, and the three laws of inheritance. Yet most students stumble here, confusing segregation with independent assortment or misinterpreting Punnett squares. The cost? Marks lost to careless mistakes when you actually understood the concept. This guide walks you through every law, every common trap, and every exam pattern so you can convert Mendelian genetics into a guaranteed scoring zone.

Understanding the Three Laws of Mendel: The Backbone of NEET Biology

Mendel's three laws—segregation, independent assortment, and dominance—form the core of NCERT Chapter 5 (Principles of Inheritance and Variation) and must be crystalline clear before you attempt any cross. Too many students memorize the definitions without grasping the mechanism, leading to confusion when questions twist the setup.

Law of Segregation: The Foundation

The Law of Segregation states that allele pairs separate during gamete formation, and each gamete receives only one allele of each gene. This is fundamental. In a monohybrid cross (Aa × Aa), each parent produces two types of gametes: A and a. When these gametes fuse randomly, you get the classic 3:1 ratio in the F₂ generation—three dominant phenotypes to one recessive.

Here's where students stumble: they forget that segregation occurs at meiosis, not at fertilization. A key tip for NEET: when a question asks "what percentage of offspring will show the recessive trait," you must trace back to the gamete formation step. If you skip that mental step, you'll confuse yourself when dealing with sex-linked traits or incomplete dominance later.

Law of Independent Assortment: Tracking Two Traits

Independent assortment applies when two or more genes are involved in different chromosome pairs. During gamete formation, the alleles of one gene segregate independently of alleles of another gene. This is why a dihybrid cross (AaBb × AaBb) yields a 9:3:3:1 ratio, not a 3:1 ratio. Many students confuse this with segregation and make the cardinal error of applying monohybrid ratios to dihybrid scenarios.

NEET examiners love testing this distinction. Expect questions like: "If two genes assort independently, what is the probability of getting AaBb offspring from AaBb × aabb?" You must recognize this as a dihybrid test cross and use the multiplication rule: (1/2 for Aa) × (1/2 for Bb) = 1/4. This type of calculation appears in almost every NEET paper.

Law of Dominance: The Phenotype Rule

Dominance determines the phenotype when heterozygous. In Aa, the A allele is dominant; the phenotype is A. This law seems simple but becomes tricky with incomplete dominance or codominance (which you'll see in Chapter 5 as well). For pure Mendelian questions, assume complete dominance unless stated otherwise.

🔴 Critical NEET Mistake #1: Confusing Genotypic and Phenotypic Ratios

In an Aa × Aa cross, the genotypic ratio is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa. But the phenotypic ratio is 3 dominant : 1 recessive. Students often reverse these or mix them up when solving word problems. Always write the genotypes first, then count phenotypes. This single habit will save you 1–2 marks per question.

Mastering Punnett Squares and Cross Predictions: Step-by-Step Strategy

NEET doesn't ask you to draw Punnett squares in the exam, but learning to construct them is essential for understanding the logic. Here's a systematic approach that works every single time.

Monohybrid Cross Procedure

  1. Identify the genotypes of parents: Write them clearly (e.g., Aa and Aa).
  2. Determine gamete types: Aa produces two gamete types: A and a (each with 50% frequency).
  3. Set up the Punnett square: 2×2 grid for monohybrid, with gametes along axes.
  4. Fill in offspring: Combine gametes. From Aa × Aa, you get AA, Aa, Aa, aa.
  5. Calculate ratios: Genotypic ratio = 1:2:1. Phenotypic ratio = 3:1 (assuming A is dominant).

Practice this with different heterozygosity levels. When you see Aa × aa (a test cross), the genotypic and phenotypic ratios are both 1:1. Examiners often use test crosses to make questions harder.

Dihybrid Cross: Where Most Students Falter

A dihybrid cross tracks two genes simultaneously. AaBb × AaBb creates a 4×4 Punnett square with 16 cells. Each parent produces four gamete types: AB, Ab, aB, ab (each 25% frequency).

  • Dominant phenotype (A_B_) = 9/16
  • Dominant for first, recessive for second (A_bb) = 3/16
  • Recessive for first, dominant for second (aaB_) = 3/16
  • Double recessive (aabb) = 1/16

This 9:3:3:1 ratio is sacred in NEET genetics. If a question gives you different phenotypic ratios (like 12:3:1), it signals incomplete dominance, epistasis, or linkage—topics beyond Mendelian basics, covered in later sections of Chapter 5. Recognizing when the 9:3:3:1 ratio breaks down is itself a high-level NEET skill.

⚡ Pro Strategy: The Multiplication Rule for Independent Events

Instead of drawing a 4×4 Punnett square for every dihybrid question, use the multiplication rule. For AaBb × AaBb, find the probability for each gene separately: P(A_) = 3/4, P(B_) = 3/4. So P(A_B_) = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. This method is faster and reduces errors in timed exams.
