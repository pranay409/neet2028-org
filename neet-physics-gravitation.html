Gravitation for NEET: Kepler's Laws and Satellite Motion Explained

By Arjun Mehta · 📅 October 15, 2026 · ⏱ 12 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Gravitation—particularly Kepler's Laws and satellite motion—appears in nearly 2-3 questions on every NEET exam, accounting for roughly 6-9 marks. This isn't a small topic; it's a pillar of Physics. Yet many students struggle because they try to memorize Kepler's Laws without understanding the orbital mechanics behind them. The result? They freeze when confronted with a question asking why geostationary satellites exist at a specific radius, or how escape velocity relates to black holes. This guide cuts through the confusion and teaches you to think like a physics topper—connecting gravity, circular motion, and energy in ways that stick on exam day.

Understanding Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation and Its Foundation

Before diving into Kepler's Laws, you must own the fundamental equation. NCERT Chapter 8 (Gravitation) introduces Newton's Law: F = GMm/r². This inverse-square relationship is non-negotiable. Every satellite problem, every orbital question, rests on this foundation.

Here's what toppers recognize: gravity decreases with the square of distance, not linearly. This means if you double your distance from Earth's center, the gravitational force becomes one-quarter as strong. NEET often tests this directly—expect questions like "If a satellite is moved to twice Earth's radius, by what factor does gravitational force change?" The answer isn't 2; it's 1/4. Students who memorize without understanding pick 1/2 and lose the mark.

Gravitational potential energy, V = -GMm/r, is equally critical. Note the negative sign—it tells you that as an object approaches a massive body, potential energy decreases (becomes more negative). This is counterintuitive but essential. When you combine gravitational PE with kinetic energy, you unlock orbital mechanics and escape velocity problems.

Key Concept: Gravitational Field Strength

The gravitational field strength g = GM/r² represents the acceleration any object experiences at distance r from a mass M. On Earth's surface, g ≈ 9.8 m/s². But at higher altitudes—say, geostationary satellite height (36,000 km above surface)—g drops significantly. NEET exams frequently ask: "What is the value of g at height h above Earth?" Answer: g = GM/(R+h)² or g = g₀ × [R/(R+h)]². Memorize this ratio formula; it appears constantly.

Kepler's Three Laws: The Orbital Rulebook

Kepler's Laws are empirical—Johannes Kepler observed planetary motion and formalized three rules. Newton later proved they emerge from his Law of Universal Gravitation. For NEET, you need to understand what each law means physically, not just recite it.

Kepler's First Law: The Law of Ellipses

All planets orbit the Sun in elliptical paths, with the Sun at one focus. For NEET purposes, most questions assume circular orbits (a special case where eccentricity = 0). But here's why the law matters: it tells you that orbits aren't random—they're geometrically constrained. The Sun doesn't sit at the center; it occupies a focus. This asymmetry means planets move faster at perihelion (closest point) and slower at aphelion (farthest point).

In satellite problems, this translates to: a satellite in an elliptical orbit will have maximum speed near perigee (closest to Earth) and minimum speed at apogee (farthest from Earth). NEET sometimes asks: "A satellite is transferred from a circular orbit to an elliptical orbit. At which point does it have maximum kinetic energy?" The answer: perigee. Why? Because total mechanical energy in the elliptical orbit equals the kinetic and potential energy sum, and at perigee, potential energy is most negative, so kinetic energy is highest.

Kepler's Second Law: The Law of Equal Areas

A line from the Sun to any planet sweeps equal areas in equal times. This is a statement of conservation of angular momentum. As a planet approaches the Sun, the distance r decreases, so velocity v must increase to keep angular momentum L = mvr constant. This law directly implies variable orbital speeds—planets are never uniformly fast or slow.

For satellite problems, this is your key to understanding why escape velocity and orbital velocity differ at the same altitude. A satellite in circular orbit maintains constant speed. But if it's given extra velocity to achieve an elliptical path (first transfer orbit), its motion obeys the area law: it covers equal areas in equal time, accelerating as it nears Earth.

Kepler's Third Law: The Law of Periods

The square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its semi-major axis: T² ∝ a³, or more precisely, T² = (4π²/GM) × a³. This is the most frequently tested Kepler's Law in NEET. Here's why it's powerful: it relates orbital radius directly to period without needing to know velocity or mass separately.

For two satellites orbiting the same body: (T₁/T₂)² = (r₁/r₂)³. If a satellite is twice as far from Earth, its period is 2^(3/2) ≈ 2.83 times longer. NEET loves this—questions like "If you place a satellite at 4 times the original radius, the period becomes how many times?" require you to compute (4)^(3/2) = 8. The formula is non-negotiable; memorize it cold.

Orbital Mechanics: Velocity, Energy, and Escape

This is where gravitation truly comes alive in NEET exams. Three velocities dominate satellite questions: orbital velocity, escape velocity, and transfer velocity.

Orbital Velocity in Circular Orbits

For a satellite in stable circular orbit, gravitational force provides centripetal force:

GMm/r² = mv²/r

Simplifying: v_orbital = √(GM/r). At Earth's surface (r = R): v = √(gR) ≈ √(10 × 6.4×10⁶) ≈ 8 km/s. At geostationary orbit (r ≈ 6.6R): v ≈ 3.1 km/s. Notice: orbital velocity decreases with altitude. This confuses many students—they think "higher orbit means faster." Wrong. Higher orbit means slower. Why? The gravitational pull is weaker, so less centripetal acceleration is needed, requiring lower speed.

Escape Velocity: Breaking Free

Escape velocity is the minimum speed needed for an object to escape a body's gravitational field entirely (reaching infinity with zero velocity). Using energy conservation:

½mv_escape² = GMm/r

So v_escape = √(2GM/r) = √2 × v_orbital. At Earth's surface: v_escape ≈ 11.2 km/s. Key insight: escape velocity is √2 times orbital velocity. NEET tests this relationship constantly. If a question gives orbital velocity and asks for escape velocity, multiply by √2. If it gives escape velocity, orbital velocity is escape/√2.

Total Mechanical Energy in Orbit

For a satellite in circular orbit:

E_total = KE + PE = ½mv² - GMm/r

Substituting v_orbital:

E_total = -GMm/(2r)

The negative sign is crucial: bound orbits have negative total energy. The more negative the energy, the deeper the orbit (closer to the body). To move a satellite from one orbit to a higher orbit requires adding energy—this is why rocket launches consume fuel. NEET often asks: "How much energy is needed to raise a satellite from radius r₁ to r₂?" Answer: ΔE = GMm/2 × (1/r₁ - 1/r₂). This formula is gold for point-grabbing questions.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Confusing Orbital and Escape Velocity

Students often assume escape velocity and orbital velocity are similar or related by a small factor. In reality, escape velocity is exactly √2 times orbital velocity at any given altitude. If orbital velocity at Earth's surface is 8 km/s, escape velocity is 11.2 km/s—not 9 or 10. Many NEET questions test this exact relationship. Memorize the √2 factor and verify it on paper before exam day.

Geostationary Satellites and Practical Applications

Geostationary satellites are a favorite NEET topic because they unify Kepler's Third Law, orbital velocity, and real-world context. A geostationary satellite orbits Earth with period T = 24 hours, remaining fixed above a single point on the equator. To find its orbital radius, use Kepler's Third Law:

T² = (4π²/GM) × r³

Solving for r with T = 86,400 s (24 hours), GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s² (Earth), you get r ≈ 42,200 km from Earth's center, or about 36,000 km above the surface. This isn't arbitrary—it's the unique radius where orbital period matches Earth's rotation.
