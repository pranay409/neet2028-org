Work, Energy and Power for NEET: Formulas and Frequently Asked Questions

By Sneha Iyer · 📅 October 14, 2026 · ⏱ 9 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Work, Energy and Power (NCERT Class 11, Chapter 6) is one of the most frequently tested topics in NEET, consistently accounting for 2–3 questions every year. What makes this chapter critical is that it bridges mechanics and thermodynamics—mastering it unlocks your ability to solve rotational dynamics, collisions, and even biochemical energy transfer problems in Biology. Yet most students treat the three concepts as isolated formulas rather than interconnected physics principles, leading to predictable calculation errors and missed conceptual questions.

This guide walks you through the exact formulas NEET expects, the hidden patterns examiners use, and the common pitfalls that cost marks even to well-prepared students.

Understanding Work: Definition and Exam Patterns

Work in physics is not effort or activity—it is the product of force and displacement in the direction of that force. The NEET exam loves testing whether you understand this strict definition.

Core Formula:

W = F · d · cos(θ)
where θ is the angle between force and displacement vectors

The angle θ is where most students slip up. If a force is perpendicular to motion (θ = 90°), work is zero—no matter how large the force. This is why friction does negative work when it opposes motion, and why a satellite moving in a circular orbit does zero work despite moving continuously.

In NEET, you'll encounter three recurring question types:

  • Inclined plane questions: An object moves up a slope. Only the component of gravitational force parallel to the slope does work. Examiners often ask for work done by gravity, normal force (always zero), and friction separately.
  • Variable force questions: A spring exerts changing force. Work = ∫F dx from initial to final position. These test calculus reasoning and appear frequently in Part A of NEET.
  • Combined system questions: Multiple objects, multiple forces. You must correctly identify which force does positive, negative, or zero work on each object.

A practical tip: always draw a free-body diagram and label the angle between each force and the displacement vector before calculating. This single habit eliminates roughly 60% of sign errors.

Energy: Kinetic and Potential Forms in NEET Context

NEET requires you to recognize energy in five forms: kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal, and chemical. But for mechanics sections, focus on kinetic and potential.

Kinetic Energy:

KE = ½ m v²

Gravitational Potential Energy:

PE = mgh (near Earth's surface)
PE = -GMm/r (in universal form)

Elastic Potential Energy (springs):

PE = ½ k x²
where k is spring constant, x is extension/compression

The Work-Energy Theorem connects these: the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy. This is not an option—it is an absolute law that NEET uses to build complex multi-step problems.

W(net) = ΔKE = KE(final) − KE(initial)

NEET examiners frequently combine this with conservation of energy: in isolated systems with no friction, total mechanical energy remains constant. If you see a problem mentioning "smooth surface" (no friction) or "ideal spring" (no energy loss), energy is conserved. Use this to bypass force calculations entirely.

⚠ Common NEET Mistake:

Students confuse "work done by a force" with "change in energy." Work done by gravity is always mgh (or mg Δh), regardless of path. But this work only equals the change in gravitational PE if no other forces do work. Always check whether friction, applied force, or spring force also acts on the object.

A classic NEET question: "A ball is thrown upward from the ground with initial velocity v. What is the net work done on the ball when it returns to the ground?" The answer is zero—because KE(final) = KE(initial), so ΔKE = 0. But students often say "mgh" (work done by gravity) because they didn't recognize the second law at work.

Power: The Rate-Based Thinking NEET Expects

Power measures how quickly energy is transferred or work is done. NEET defines three formulas, but only one is typically used in problems:

P = W / t (average power)
P = F · v · cos(θ) (instantaneous power)
P = dW / dt (differential form, rarely needed in NEET)

The second formula is critical: instantaneous power depends on both the force applied and the velocity at that instant. This is why lifting a heavy load slowly uses less power than lifting it quickly—same work, less time, higher power output.

NEET uses power to test understanding of:

  • Efficiency: Useful power output divided by total power input. A motor with 80% efficiency converts 80% of input power into mechanical work; the rest becomes heat.
  • Energy consumption in time: Energy = Power × Time. If a device runs at 100 W for 2 hours, it consumes 200 Wh or 0.2 kWh.
  • Force-velocity relationships: At constant power, if velocity increases, force must decrease (and vice versa). This explains why cars downshift under heavy load—to maintain power while reducing velocity.

A 2-mark NEET question might ask: "A motor lifts 10 kg of water at 2 m/s. If the motor's efficiency is 50%, how much power must the motor supply?" Solution: Useful power to lift = mgh/t = mgv = 10 × 10 × 2 = 200 W. Total power required = 200 / 0.5 = 400 W.

Solving Multi-Concept NEET Problems: Your Strategy

Real NEET questions rarely test one concept in isolation. A typical 3-mark problem might involve an object sliding down a slope with friction, compressing a spring at the bottom, then rebounding. You must identify which sections use work calculations, which use energy conservation, and which require power reasoning.

Your approach:

  1. Identify the system: Is it isolated (use energy conservation) or are external forces doing work (use work-energy theorem)?
  2. Choose your method: Force-based (F = ma, kinematics) or energy-based (work, KE, PE)? Energy is usually faster if the question asks about velocity or height at specific points.
  3. Account for all energy transfers: Friction converts mechanical energy to heat (always subtract). Springs store and release elastic PE. Moving parts carry KE.
  4. Check your signs: Positive work increases energy. Negative work (friction, object moving against applied force) decreases energy.
  5. Verify dimensional analysis: Work and energy in joules, power in watts. If your answer has wrong units, recalculate.

A worked example: "A 2 kg block starts from rest at the top of a 5 m frictionless ramp (angle 30°). At the bottom, it compresses a spring by 0.5 m before stopping. Find the spring constant k."

Solution: PE at top = mgh = 2 × 10 × 5 = 100 J. This converts entirely to elastic PE at maximum compression (no friction). Elastic PE = ½kx² = 100, so ½k(0.5)² = 100, giving k = 800 N/m. Using energy conservation bypasses all force and acceleration calculations.
